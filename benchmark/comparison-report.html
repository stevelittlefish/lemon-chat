Home battery without solar — model & algorithm comparison

The same research prompt was run through five models, on two versions of the research engine. This report compares the models against each other, and the original algorithm against v2.

The task. A UK household using ~9,000 kWh/yr on a flat 24.7p/kWh tariff, with a high daytime home-office load and no plans for solar, asks whether a home battery is financially worthwhile. Compare four options — stay flat-rate, switch to time-of-use (ToU) without a battery, add a ~5 kWh battery, add a ~10 kWh battery — with payback, 10-year net outcome, and sensitivity analysis. It is a hard, numeric, citation-heavy question with a genuinely non-obvious answer.

Headline finding

On substance, both algorithm versions agree, and so do 4 of the 5 models: a battery does not pay back within 10 years; the best financial move is switching tariff, not buying hardware. The answer is robust to the algorithm change.

The algorithm change mainly fixed efficiency, not conclusions. In the old engine the reasoning models (gpt-5.6-luna, gpt-5.6-terra) never self-terminated — they ran to the 8-round ceiling every time. In v2 the stop-check fires, so they stop when the evidence is sufficient. That roughly halved runtime and cost for the expensive models with no loss of quality.

Reading the table. The old engine's two heaviest runs — gpt-5.6-terra at $3.76 and gpt-5.6-luna at $1.60 — both hit the 8-round maximum, meaning they never decided they were done; they were cut off by the ceiling. gemma likewise ran 6 rounds. In v2 every model stops on its own terms (meta.json → stop_reason), and terra's run even ended on "consecutive empty rounds — writing report with findings so far", the exact safety-valve the stop-check provides. The result is a 33–64% cut in wall-clock time and up to 62% cut in cost for the reasoning models, while the cheap/fast models (gemini, claude) were already terminating early and barely moved.

Why this maps to a real fix. The v2 runs were produced after the commit "Fix research stop-check never firing with reasoning worker models." gpt-5.6-luna and gpt-5.6-terra are reasoning models; in the old engine they exhibit exactly the broken-stop-check symptom (always run to the round ceiling). In v2 they stop early. This is the single clearest behavioural difference between the two datasets.

The consensus (8 of 10 runs)

  • A standalone battery does not repay its capital within 10 years through tariff arbitrage at realistic installed prices (£4k for 5 kWh, £5.5–6.5k for 10 kWh). Paybacks cluster at 15–32 years.
  • The highest-value action is a tariff change, which is free and reversible. The battery is a secondary, contingent decision that only works with unusually low install quotes and/or a wide, durable price spread.
  • The household's daytime-heavy, inflexible load is a structural disadvantage — a point every capable model raised: a battery earns most when it displaces expensive evening peak imports, and this household's demand is midday.

The two genuine divergences

1. gemma4-31b is the persistent outlier — in both versions. It alone concludes the 10 kWh battery is a clear winner (+£1,300 old, +£2,700 v2, 6.7–8.2 yr payback). The error is the same in both runs: it credits the battery with a near-daily full cycle at the full peak-to-offpeak spread (~£730–820/yr) while under-charging the household for the large daytime load the battery cannot buffer. The algorithm change did not fix this — it's a model-reasoning limitation, and v2 gemma is actually more over-optimistic than old gemma.

2. "Does switching tariff alone save money?" is the real point of contention — and it is driven by a modelling assumption, not the engine. Models that assume a 2-tier ToU tariff with a punitive ~30p daytime rate (old claude, both geminis) find switching costs this daytime-heavy household money. Models that assume a 3-tier tariff with a benign ~24.5p shoulder rate and a narrow evening peak (both GPTs, and v2 claude) find switching saves £150–330/yr. This is the single largest sensitivity in the whole question, and the better reports (gpt-terra, v2 claude) call it out explicitly.

Notable old→v2 shift: claude flipped the tariff verdict

The clearest content change between versions is claude: the old run modelled Octopus Go at 8.5p/30.1p and concluded switching tariff would add £270/yr; the v2 run used the actual price-cap-aligned 7p/26.11p figures and concluded switching saves £100–320/yr — moving claude into line with the gpt consensus that "switch tariff first" is the answer. This is a swing in the recommended action, though it stems from the tariff data selected in each run rather than a proven, deterministic engine change, so treat it as suggestive rather than causal.

4 · v2 engine also produces far richer telemetry

Beyond the conclusions, the v2 runs ship a full audit trail that the old runs lack — consistent with the engine's checkpoint-and-resume design:

Per-round reports
✓

reports/NNN-round-NN.md — the evolving report after each round, plus plan.txt.

State snapshots
✓

snapshots/*.json — full checkpoint after planning and every round (crash recovery).

Event stream
✓

events.jsonl — timestamped phase log: planning → searching → reading → analyzing → deciding → writing.

The event logs are what let us confirm the round story precisely: gpt-5.6-luna logged 7 deciding phases across 7 rounds, gpt-5.6-terra logged a warning phase (the empty-round trip) before writing, and the fast models logged a single decide-then-write. The old runs expose only the final report and an info.json summary.

5 · Verdict on the algorithm change

v2 is a clear improvement, and a low-risk one. It preserves the substantive answer (battery not worthwhile; switch tariff) while fixing the reasoning-model stop-check that was burning rounds, time and money. For the two most expensive models it cut cost by 44–62% and time by 33–48% with no quality regression, and it adds the checkpoint/telemetry needed for crash recovery and debugging.

What v2 did not fix, and can't: gemma4-31b's over-optimistic battery arithmetic is a model limitation, unchanged across versions. And the biggest source of answer-to-answer variation — whether a bare tariff switch helps — comes from the tariff assumptions each run picks up, not from the engine. If more consistency is wanted there, the lever is prompting the model to state and sensitivity-test its assumed peak/shoulder/off-peak structure, which the strongest reports already do voluntarily.
