Is a home battery worthwhile without solar panels?

For this 9,000 kWh home with substantial daytime home-office use, a grid-charged battery can work technically—but is unlikely to repay its installed cost under ordinary central-case assumptions.

Bottom line

Change tariff first; buy storage only on measured evidence.

The strongest low-risk choice is a competitive flat tariff. A suitable time-of-use tariff is worth testing, particularly if its cheap period overlaps with daytime office demand. If a battery is still attractive after analysing 12 months of half-hourly data, the approximately 5 kWh system is preferable to 10 kWh—but neither is financially compelling at the assumed installed prices.

How a battery without solar makes money

The battery’s main financial mechanism is simple: buy electricity cheaply, store it, and use it when imports are expensive. The relevant value is the price spread after losses—not the peak import price itself.

1 kWh delivered ÷ 0.88 round-trip efficiency = 1.136 kWh bought 30p peak − (10p ÷ 0.88) = 18.64p gross saving per delivered kWh

That 18.64p margin must cover capital cost, unused capacity, standby consumption, degradation, maintenance and eventual replacement.

Why this household is difficult to optimise

The office uses 10–20 kWh per day, but much of it is consumed during daytime working hours. A battery charged overnight helps only if it has enough stored energy, can discharge during that period, has sufficient inverter power, and does not empty before a more expensive evening window. Direct overnight use may also be cheaper than routing that electricity through a battery.

Flexible consumption can reduce peak exposure, as recognised in Ofgem and government material [S54] [S53]. Government consultation material cites savings of more than £200 per year for some households avoiding peak periods, but that conditional figure should not be applied automatically here [S56] [S58].

Central assumptions

Household profile

  • England; assessment date 14 July 2026
  • 9,000 kWh annual electricity use
  • Current flat rate: 24.7p/kWh
  • Gas heating; smart meter; homeowner
  • High daytime office demand; limited manual shifting
  • No solar expected during the 10-year period
  • Financial savings valued above backup power

Model inputs

  • Standing charge: £200/year
  • Competitive flat: 22.85p/kWh
  • TOU: 10p overnight, 30p peak, 24p shoulder
  • Consumption shares: 20% / 25% / 55%
  • Round-trip efficiency: 88%
  • Degradation: 2% annually
  • Installed prices: £4,500 / £7,000

The current-rate baseline is 9,000 × £0.247 + £200 = £2,423. The July–September 2026 price-cap benchmark is approximately 26.11p/kWh and 57.19p/day, or about £2,559 at 9,000 kWh including standing charge [S40]. A competitive tariff may be cheaper, but postcode-specific quotes are essential. Market pages cite rates beginning around 22.85p/kWh, while some regional fixed examples around 27.78p/kWh are worse than the assumed current rate [S3].

Assessing the choices

Option 1

Competitive flat tariff

£2,256.50 calculated as 9,000 × £0.2285 + £200. Rounded, this is £2,257—about £167 below the current baseline.

It requires no capital, installation, control platform or battery warranty. Its saving is independent of consumption timing, making it a strong fit for a daytime-heavy profile.

Weakness: it does not exploit unusually cheap overnight or dynamic prices, and tariff renewal may change the result.

Option 2

TOU tariff without a battery

The model’s weighted rate is:

(20% × 10p) + (25% × 30p) + (55% × 24p) = 22.7p/kWh

That produces £2,243/year—only about £13.50 below the competitive flat option. It is reversible and avoids battery losses.

A cheap daytime period may suit the office better than overnight-only pricing. EDF has described an amber period of roughly 6am–4pm [S42]. However, smart tariffs can cost more when substantial use remains in peak periods [S2].

Option 3

Approximately 5 kWh battery

Assuming 4.5 kWh usable capacity and 300 equivalent cycles:

4.5 kWh × 300 = 1,350 kWh delivered

At 18.64p per delivered kWh, the first-year battery-specific saving is approximately £252. Payback at £4,500 is approximately 17.9 years.

It is more likely to be used regularly than a larger system, but remains outside the 10-year financial horizon in the central case.

Option 4

Approximately 10 kWh battery

Assuming 8 kWh usable capacity and 220 equivalent cycles:

8 kWh × 220 = 1,760 kWh delivered

That creates approximately £328 of first-year battery saving. At £7,000, simple payback is approximately 21.3 years.

Filling it requires about 9.09 kWh of charging electricity. With only around 4.9 kWh/day of overnight consumption on average, much of its capacity may be unused on many days.

Why the lowest annual bill is not the best investment

After 2% annual degradation, approximate ten-year gross battery savings are £2,306 for 5 kWh and £3,001 for 10 kWh. Against the competitive flat tariff, the TOU-only option is around £135 better; the 5 kWh system is around £2,059 worse; and the 10 kWh system around £3,864 worse.

Other sensitivities

Peak share

At 15% peak use, the illustrative TOU average is 22.1p/kWh; at 25%, 22.7p; at 35%, 23.3p. At 35%, TOU is already more expensive than the 22.85p flat benchmark before a battery.

Efficiency

At 88%, 1.14 kWh must be bought per delivered kWh. At 80%, it is 1.25 kWh; at 70%, 1.43 kWh. Usable AC-side energy matters more than headline capacity.

Degradation

At 4% annual degradation, average annual battery savings fall to about £209 for 5 kWh and £272 for 10 kWh. Maintenance, monitoring or a £2,000 inverter replacement materially worsens results.

Shared considerations before buying

Tariff eligibility

Some cheap overnight tariffs are designed for EVs and may require a vehicle or charger [S20] [S49]. Some battery tariffs require solar, an export MPAN or approved equipment [S22] [S24] [S25]. Obtain written confirmation that grid charging and household discharge are permitted.

VAT and incentives

HMRC guidance indicates that qualifying energy-saving-material installations may receive 0% VAT during the relevant period, important before 31 March 2027 [S26]. Installer commentary argues that standalone batteries can qualify without solar [S59]; verify the precise statutory basis and invoice treatment.

Exports and FIT

The Feed-in Tariff closed to new applicants on 1 April 2019, so a new standalone battery should not be treated as FIT-eligible [S55]. Do not assume SEG or flexibility income for grid-charged exports; model zero unless a supplier confirms eligibility [S38].

Power and backup

Capacity is measured in kWh; inverter delivery capability is measured in kW. A 10 kWh battery with a 2.5 kW inverter cannot supply a simultaneous 4–5 kW load. Standard grid-connected systems may not work in a power cut; backup needs a gateway, changeover equipment, protected circuits and additional cost.

Warranty and replacement

Check grid-arbitrage permission, throughput or cycle limits, retained capacity, inverter cover, labour, call-outs, software support and replacement arrangements. The central model assumes no maintenance and no replacement, which favours the battery.

Direct demand matters

More total consumption does not automatically mean more battery value. Extra usage improves the case only when it occurs during the profitable discharge window and can be physically served by the battery.

When the conclusion could change

A battery becomes plausible if most of these are true

  • Off-peak electricity is consistently around 5–8p/kWh.
  • Peak electricity is reliably 35–45p/kWh or higher.
  • The spread persists for much of the battery’s useful life.
  • The home has substantial 4pm–7pm or evening demand.
  • A 5 kWh system can complete roughly 300–350 useful cycles annually.
  • The additional 10 kWh capacity is regularly used if selected.
  • The inverter can meet simultaneous household demand.
  • Installation is around £2,500–£3,000 for 5 kWh, or materially below £7,000 for 10 kWh.
  • Warranty terms allow grid arbitrage and degradation is modest.

A cheap daytime period matching the office load would strengthen the tariff-only case. Conversely, the battery case weakens when overnight demand is already high, peak consumption is low or inflexible, a large backup reserve is required, or cheap hours do not match the household’s operating pattern.

A practical decision process

1Measure

Download at least 12 months of half-hourly import data.

2Compare

Model a competitive flat tariff and complete TOU schedules.

3Quote

Obtain at least three fully itemised battery quotations.

4Verify

Require an installer simulation using the home’s actual data.

Consumption data to request

  • Weekday and weekend profiles
  • Overnight and 4pm–7pm use
  • Office load by hour
  • Maximum half-hourly import
  • Seasonal variation
  • Flexible appliances and direct overnight use
  • Demand physically serviceable by storage

Tariff information to obtain

  • Every import rate and exact time band
  • Standing charge, VAT and contract length
  • Eligibility and exit fees
  • Grid-charging permission
  • EV, solar or approved-equipment requirements
  • Supplier-control and software terms
  • Export rules and rate-change mechanisms

Battery quotation must show

  • Nominal and usable capacity
  • Continuous and peak charge/discharge power
  • AC-side efficiency and standby consumption
  • Inverter rating and DNO work
  • Minimum state of charge and backup circuits
  • Installation, VAT and electrical modifications

Warranty and modelling checks

  • End-of-warranty retained capacity
  • Throughput and cycle limits
  • Maintenance and software fees
  • Inverter and battery replacement assumptions
  • Actual state-of-charge simulation
  • Degradation, reserve and periods of under-use

Reject optimistic estimates

Do not accept a calculation that assumes one complete charge-discharge cycle every day without demonstrating that the tariff windows, charging energy, load profile, usable capacity and power limits support it.

Final verdict

A grid-charged battery without solar can save money, but this home’s high daytime office demand and limited flexibility make frequent profitable cycling uncertain. Under the central assumptions, neither battery repays its capital cost within ten years.
