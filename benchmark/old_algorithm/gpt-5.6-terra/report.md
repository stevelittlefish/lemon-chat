# Is a Home Battery Worthwhile Without Solar Panels for a UK Home?

## Executive summary

For this high-electricity-use household in England, installing a home battery **without solar panels is unlikely to be financially worthwhile on central assumptions as of 14 July 2026**.

The best financial result is likely to come from changing electricity tariff first, without buying a battery. At 9,000 kWh/year, even a modest reduction in the household’s effective electricity price matters: every 1p/kWh reduction is worth roughly **£90 per year**.

A suitable time-of-use (TOU), Tracker-style or competitive flat tariff could plausibly save around **£150–£290 per year** compared with the current 24.7p/kWh flat tariff, without upfront hardware costs, battery losses, degradation, maintenance risk or uncertain long-term tariff economics.

A 5 kWh battery might save about **£186/year** through tariff arbitrage under a reasonably favourable illustrative tariff spread, but would cost around **£4,250 installed**, implying a simple payback of around **23 years**. A 10 kWh battery could save around **£322/year**, but at a central installed cost of **£6,500**, still implies roughly **20 years** simple payback.

The household’s large daytime home-office load does not automatically improve battery economics. It helps only where that consumption occurs during genuinely expensive periods. If a suitable TOU tariff makes daytime electricity relatively cheap, direct daytime use is better than buying electricity overnight, storing it, and losing roughly 10% in the battery.

The prudent sequence is therefore:

1. Obtain 12 months of half-hourly smart-meter data.
2. Compare competitive flat tariffs, dynamic tariffs and daytime-friendly TOU tariffs.
3. Trial a tariff without a battery, ideally one with no exit fee.
4. Consider a battery only if actual consumption data shows consistently high demand during expensive tariff periods and a supplier confirms favourable battery-compatible tariff terms.

## Assumptions and household context

The household uses around **9,000 kWh/year**, materially above normal domestic consumption benchmarks. OVO cites Ofgem benchmarks of around 3,800 kWh/year for a high unrestricted electricity user and 6,100 kWh/year for a high Economy 7 user; this household is therefore unusually electricity-intensive. [S59]

At the existing 24.7p/kWh flat rate:

\[
9,000 \times £0.247 = £2,223/year
\]

before standing charges.

The home office uses approximately 10–20 kWh/day, mainly during daytime working hours. That makes the demand profile unusual. It is not enough to know that annual usage is high: battery economics depend on **when** the electricity is used.

A battery can create value only when it stores low-priced electricity and later avoids electricity imports that would otherwise have been expensive. If daytime office consumption occurs during a cheap tariff period, the battery adds little value. It is normally better to use electricity directly at, say, 15–20p/kWh than to buy it overnight at 9p/kWh, store it with losses, and discharge it later.

Standing charges matter when comparing suppliers, but are less important than unit rates for a household using 9,000 kWh/year. Octopus warns that households using more than 1,800 kWh/year will generally lose more from high unit rates than they gain from low standing charges. [S56]

## Option 1: Remain on the existing flat tariff without a battery

### Indicative cost

At 24.7p/kWh, annual energy-unit costs are approximately:

\[
9,000 \times 24.7p = £2,223
\]

This is predictable and simple, but it is not obviously competitive for a high-use household.

### Strengths

The main advantage is certainty. There is no behavioural change, no tariff monitoring, no capital investment and no concern about battery efficiency, degradation, warranty restrictions or repair costs.

There is also no risk that a dynamic tariff produces unusually expensive days. Tracker-style tariffs can have price ceilings as high as 100p/kWh, so risk tolerance matters for a household importing 9,000 kWh/year. [S17]

### Weaknesses

The central weakness is opportunity cost. If the household can reduce its effective average price by only 1p/kWh, it saves:

\[
9,000 \times £0.01 = £90/year
\]

A reduction from 24.7p/kWh to 23.0p/kWh would save around £153/year before standing-charge differences.

### Ideal use case

This remains suitable for someone who values price predictability more than savings or who cannot access a genuinely competitive tariff. Financially, however, it is unlikely to be the best available option.

## Option 2: Change tariff without installing a battery

### Why tariff switching is likely to be the biggest opportunity

The household already has the most important technical prerequisite for smart tariffs: a smart meter capable of providing half-hourly readings. Suppliers use that data to offer TOU or dynamic tariffs, and customers can request half-hourly data to analyse their consumption pattern. [S31]

A competitive flat tariff at approximately 23.0p/kWh would cost:

\[
9,000 \times £0.230 = £2,070/year
\]

That is a saving of:

\[
£2,223 - £2,070 = £153/year
\]

with no equipment cost.

A well-matched TOU tariff might achieve an effective annual average cost around 21.5p/kWh in this model:

\[
9,000 \times £0.215 = £1,935/year
\]

That would save around:

\[
£2,223 - £1,935 = £288/year
\]

again without any battery investment.

### Why daytime-friendly tariffs may suit the home office

The most interesting tariff structure for this household is not necessarily one with only cheap overnight electricity. E.ON Next Smart Saver has described a structure with:

- super-off-peak electricity around 2am–5am;
- off-peak electricity around 5am–4pm;
- peak electricity around 4pm–7pm;
- off-peak electricity again around 7pm–2am. [S48][S57]

That pattern is important because much of the office demand occurs during daytime working hours. If the office electricity is billed at an off-peak rate between 5am and 4pm, the household could benefit directly without battery losses.

This is the key reason a battery should not be assumed to be helpful merely because the household has high daytime demand. Daytime consumption helps a battery only if daytime prices are high. On a daytime-friendly tariff, the office load may strengthen the case for tariff switching but weaken the case for storage.

### Tariffs that should not be assumed available

Cheap EV tariffs are often used in battery marketing calculations, but they should not be treated as available to a household without an eligible EV.

For example:

- Octopus Go requires an electric car charged at home. [S15]
- Intelligent Octopus Go and related products are EV-focused. [S2]
- E.ON Next Drive Smart advertises cheap overnight rates but requires eligible EV and charger arrangements. [S32][S57]
- British Gas’s 9p/kWh overnight EV tariff also requires EV-related eligibility. [S38]
- Octopus Flux and Intelligent Flux are designed for homes with solar and batteries, not standalone battery households. [S14][S20]
- E.ON Next Pumped is aimed at heat-pump households and is not an appropriate assumed tariff for this gas-heated home. [S19]

The lesson is straightforward: a cheap overnight rate is irrelevant if the household cannot actually access the tariff on its intended terms.

### Ideal use case

This is the strongest option for a high-use household that can access a competitive flat tariff, daytime-friendly TOU tariff, Tracker or dynamic tariff and is willing to monitor bills for a few months.

## Option 3: Install an approximately 5 kWh battery and switch tariff

### Technical assumptions

A nominal 5 kWh system rarely provides exactly 5 kWh of usable delivered electricity. This model assumes:

- nominal capacity: 5.0 kWh;
- usable battery capacity: 4.5 kWh;
- AC round-trip efficiency: 90%;
- delivered energy from a full useful cycle: 4.05 kWh;
- useful cycles: 300/year;
- average ten-year output reduction from degradation: 10%;
- average annual delivered energy over ten years: about 1,094 kWh/year.

The assumed installed price is **£4,250**, with a plausible market range around £3,000–£5,500 depending on inverter, installation complexity, backup equipment and electrical work. [S10][S53][S68]

A current market example cited for context is an Enphase 5 kWh system at around £4,100–£4,600 installed, with 3.2 kW rated output and a stated 15-year product warranty. [S68]

### Why the battery is not assumed to cycle fully every day

A simplistic battery model might assume one full charge and discharge every day. That would be too optimistic here.

Some overnight electricity will power ordinary household consumption directly. That energy cannot simultaneously charge the battery. A short overnight cheap window may also limit charging, especially if the home has meaningful overnight loads.

The battery might also discharge into daytime office demand before the highest-price evening period begins. If the tariff’s peak is only 4pm–7pm, a battery may not have enough high-value demand to use all of its capacity at the most valuable times.

### Financial model

The illustrative battery-arbitrage tariff assumes:

- battery charging electricity: 9p/kWh;
- avoided high-price import: 27p/kWh;
- round-trip efficiency: 90%.

The cost of each delivered kWh is:

\[
\frac{9p}{0.90} = 10p/kWh
\]

The gross saving per delivered battery kWh is therefore:

\[
27p - 10p = 17p/kWh
\]

At 1,094 kWh/year of useful delivered energy:

\[
1,094 \times £0.17 = £186/year
\]

The illustrative tariff without battery would cost roughly £2,106/year. With the 5 kWh battery, the annual cost falls to around £1,920/year.

However, the relevant comparison is the same tariff without the battery. The battery itself creates only around £186/year of extra saving.

### Payback

\[
\frac{£4,250}{£186/year} = 22.9\ years
\]

This is before maintenance, inverter failure, battery replacement, finance costs or the time value of money. It is therefore not attractive as a ten-year investment.

### Ideal use case

A 5 kWh battery becomes potentially reasonable only where:

- total installed price is unusually low, perhaps near £3,000;
- the household has at least 3–5 kWh of expensive-period demand on most days;
- a genuinely battery-compatible cheap charging tariff is available;
- the supplier explicitly allows grid charging and automated battery control;
- the inverter has sufficient power to meet relevant loads;
- backup power has separate personal value.

## Option 4: Install an approximately 10 kWh battery and switch tariff

### Technical assumptions

The 10 kWh model assumes:

- nominal capacity: 10.0 kWh;
- initial usable capacity: 9.0 kWh;
- 90% AC round-trip efficiency;
- 8.10 kWh delivered from a complete usable cycle;
- 260 useful cycles/year;
- 10% average degradation allowance over ten years;
- average delivered energy around 1,895 kWh/year.

The central installed price is **£6,500**, with a wide plausible range around £5,000–£8,500. [S10][S41][S53]

An illustrative AlphaESS example has 10.01 kWh nominal capacity, 9.59 kWh usable capacity, 5 kW maximum charge/discharge capability and a ten-year warranty, with indicative installed pricing of £5,850–£6,850. [S68]

### Capacity is not the same as useful output

A larger battery is not necessarily better. The household must have enough expensive-period demand to empty it.

If a tariff peak lasts from 4pm–7pm, a 10 kWh battery must either:

1. discharge around 3.3 kW continuously for three hours, or  
2. continue discharging into later high-priced periods.

If the property’s demand during the expensive window is lower, the battery remains partly full and earns less than expected. If the inverter output is too low, the household may still import electricity at the peak tariff while the battery is operating.

The 10 kWh system therefore has a greater risk of under-use than the smaller battery.

### Financial model and payback

At the same assumed 17p/kWh delivered arbitrage saving:

\[
1,895 \times £0.17 = £322/year
\]

The annual energy cost falls to roughly £1,784 in the illustrative battery-tariff scenario.

But the battery’s simple payback is:

\[
\frac{£6,500}{£322/year} = 20.2\ years
\]

This is slightly better than the 5 kWh system in simple-payback terms, but still far outside the stated ten-year ownership horizon.

### Ideal use case

A 10 kWh battery may make sense where the home has very high and consistent expensive-period demand, an electric vehicle, heat pump, electric heating, frequent power cuts or a strong desire for backup power. It is difficult to justify for tariff arbitrage alone in this gas-heated household.

## Shared Considerations

### Battery degradation, warranties and replacement risk

Battery performance declines over time. The central model already reduces average output by 10% over the ten-year period. If average output fell by 20% instead, annual battery-arbitrage savings would fall from around £186 to £165 for the 5 kWh battery and from £322 to around £286 for the 10 kWh battery.

The financial model also assumes no inverter failure. That is optimistic. A £1,500 inverter replacement would directly worsen the ten-year result by £1,500.

Warranties should be assessed carefully. A “ten-year warranty” does not necessarily mean ten years of full usable capacity, unrestricted cycling, free labour or inverter cover. The homeowner should inspect:

- retained-capacity guarantee;
- throughput or cycle limits;
- whether grid charging is permitted;
- exclusions for high-frequency cycling;
- inverter warranty duration;
- labour, removal and reinstallation cover;
- backup-operation conditions;
- remote-control and internet requirements.

Consumer and forum sources broadly support allowing for roughly 10–15% storage losses and long-term capacity decline, but those sources are not substitutes for the actual manufacturer warranty for the quoted system. [S64]

### Battery power rating matters as much as kWh capacity

Capacity is measured in kWh; power is measured in kW. A 10 kWh battery with a 3 kW inverter cannot supply more than 3 kW continuously, even if it still has plenty of energy stored.

The home office may be modest in power terms, but coincident cooking, laundry, kettle, dishwasher, immersion heater or other household loads can exceed the inverter rating. The house then imports electricity at the peak rate even while the battery is discharging.

A quotation should specify continuous and peak charge/discharge power, not merely battery capacity.

### VAT, grants and market rules

Qualifying residential battery storage supplied and installed before 1 April 2027 can receive 0% VAT, including standalone batteries that store electricity imported from the grid. [S21][S22]

That is helpful, but it is not a grant. The homeowner should ensure the quote explicitly states the VAT treatment and total installed price. Separately purchased equipment may not receive the same VAT treatment.

There is no general England-wide grant that should be assumed for an ordinary standalone home battery. Government policy has discussed finance for solar and batteries, but no household-specific support should be included unless the homeowner receives written confirmation. [S23]

No Smart Export Guarantee income is included. SEG applies to electricity exported from eligible renewable generation; it should not be assumed to pay for electricity bought from the grid, stored in a standalone battery and exported later. [S61]

The installer should also confirm Distribution Network Operator requirements, including whether G98 notification or G99 approval applies, whether export limitation is needed, and whether the consumer unit requires upgrading. [S11]

### Backup power is valuable, but not free

A battery can provide resilience during outages only if the system is designed for islanding or backup operation. That commonly requires a backup gateway, critical-load consumer unit or dedicated circuits. Backup equipment may add around £400–£700 in some installer examples, but the actual cost depends heavily on the house and scope. [S53]

Backup should be valued separately from bill savings. A battery might be personally worthwhile for resilience even if its tariff-arbitrage return is poor.

### Tariff spread sensitivity

At 90% round-trip efficiency, the saving per delivered kWh is:

\[
\text{Avoided import rate} - \frac{\text{Charging rate}}{0.90}
\]

| Overnight charge price | Avoided import price | Saving per delivered kWh |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 9p | 24.7p | 14.7p |
| 9p | 27p | 17.0p |
| 9p | 35p | 25.0p |
| 9p | 45p | 35.0p |
| 12p | 27p | 13.7p |
| 12p | 35p | 21.7p |

For the 5 kWh battery to repay £4,250 over ten years at 1,094 kWh/year delivered, it would need around **38.8p/kWh** of battery-arbitrage value. With 9p charging, that implies avoiding imports at nearly **48.8p/kWh** on average.

For the 10 kWh battery to repay £6,500 over ten years at 1,895 kWh/year delivered, it needs about **34.3p/kWh** of benefit per delivered kWh. With 9p charging, that implies avoiding imports around **44.3p/kWh** on average.

Such prices can occur occasionally on dynamic tariffs, but the difficult requirement is avoiding them reliably, at enough volume, for a decade.

### Peak-period demand sensitivity

At 9,000 kWh/year:

| Share of annual use during expensive period | Expensive-period electricity | Average per day |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 10% | 900 kWh/year | 2.5 kWh/day |
| 20% | 1,800 kWh/year | 4.9 kWh/day |
| 25% | 2,250 kWh/year | 6.2 kWh/day |
| 30% | 2,700 kWh/year | 7.4 kWh/day |

The 5 kWh system’s assumed annual delivered energy requires roughly 12% of annual use to fall in valuable discharge periods. The 10 kWh system requires roughly 21%.

That means a 10 kWh battery is likely oversized if less than one-fifth of household electricity is consumed in genuinely expensive periods.

## What data and quotations to obtain before buying

Before making a decision, the homeowner should obtain the following.

### Half-hourly consumption data

Download at least 12 months of smart-meter data and separate:

- weekday versus weekend demand;
- winter versus summer demand;
- 00:00–05:00 overnight usage;
- 05:00–16:00 daytime office usage;
- 16:00–19:00 peak-period usage;
- 19:00–00:00 evening usage;
- highest half-hourly and hourly demand.

The most important question is: how many kWh are imported during the supplier’s most expensive periods on a normal weekday?

### Tariff information

Request postcode-specific written tariff information showing:

- unit rate in every time band;
- standing charge;
- tariff duration;
- exit fees;
- eligibility conditions;
- whether grid charging is permitted;
- whether battery automation is permitted;
- whether rates may change;
- whether dynamic tariffs have a price cap;
- whether battery export is permitted or restricted.

### Battery quotations

Obtain at least three itemised quotations showing:

- total installed cost including VAT;
- nominal and usable capacity;
- continuous charge and discharge kW;
- AC round-trip efficiency;
- standby power consumption;
- warranty years;
- end-of-warranty capacity guarantee;
- throughput and cycle limits;
- inverter warranty;
- backup hardware and critical-load scope;
- DNO/G98/G99 work;
- consumer-unit upgrades;
- monitoring subscriptions;
- labour and making-good;
- likely replacement cost after warranty.

## Best For verdicts

**Best for lowest risk:** Staying on a competitive flat tariff, because it provides predictable pricing and requires no investment.

**Best for financial savings:** A well-matched TOU, Tracker or competitive flat tariff without a battery, because it can save approximately £150–£290/year without capital cost or battery losses.

**Best for someone who values backup power:** A carefully designed 5 kWh battery system, but only if resilience is worth paying for separately from bill savings.

**Best for very high expensive-period loads:** A 10 kWh battery only where half-hourly data proves regular high-price demand of roughly 6–8 kWh/day and the battery has enough inverter power to serve it.

**Best for this household:** Trial a suitable tariff first, then reassess with real half-hourly data.

## Conclusion

A home battery without solar panels is **not likely to pay for itself within ten years for this household on central assumptions**.

A roughly 5 kWh battery has an indicative simple payback of about **23 years**. A roughly 10 kWh battery improves annual savings somewhat, but its larger capital cost and greater risk of under-use still produce a simple payback of around **20 years**. Both results are before repairs, inverter replacement, financing, paid maintenance and the time value of money.

Changing tariff without buying a battery is much more likely to provide meaningful savings. At 9,000 kWh/year, a competitive flat tariff could save around **£153/year**, while a well-matched TOU tariff could save around **£288/year**. Over ten years, this is a positive financial outcome without hardware risk.

The conclusion changes only if several favourable conditions coincide: a low installed battery price, written eligibility for a very cheap battery-compatible tariff, consistently high expensive-period demand, sufficient inverter output, strong warranty terms and a durable price spread large enough to justify storage losses and capital cost.

The strongest argument against the recommendation is that the household’s very high consumption could make a cheap battery attractive if it can consistently charge at around 7–9p/kWh and displace imports above roughly 40–45p/kWh. But that case must be proven with half-hourly data and written tariff terms. It should not be assumed from EV tariffs, solar-battery marketing illustrations or full-cycle-per-day claims.

For financial savings alone, the best choice is: **switch tariff first; do not buy a battery unless measured data and firm quotations demonstrate a much stronger case than the central model.**
