UK household decision brief · England · 14 July 2026

Is a home battery worthwhile without solar panels?

A practical comparison for a high-electricity-use home: 9,000 kWh/year, a daytime home office and no planned solar installation.

Bottom line: switch tariff first. On central assumptions, a standalone battery is unlikely to repay itself within ten years. A well-matched tariff can plausibly save £150–£290 a year without hardware cost, losses, degradation or long-term battery risk.

Research scope: tariff comparison, grid-charged battery arbitrage and household-specific sizing considerations.

Executive decision

The tariff is the larger, lower-risk lever

At 9,000 kWh/year, every 1p/kWh reduction in the household’s effective electricity price is worth about £90 per year. The large daytime office load helps storage only if it coincides with genuinely expensive tariff periods; if daytime power is already cheap, direct use is preferable to charging overnight and losing energy in a battery.

Why usage timing matters

High annual use does not automatically mean strong battery economics

The office uses approximately 10–20 kWh/day, mainly in daytime working hours. Storage creates value only by buying low-priced electricity and avoiding imports that would otherwise be expensive.

If daytime office consumption is billed at a low TOU rate, use it directly. It is usually better to use electricity at 15–20p/kWh than buy it at 9p/kWh overnight, store it and lose roughly 10% before use.

For a high-use household, unit rates matter more than standing charges. Octopus notes that customers using above 1,800 kWh/year generally lose more from a high unit rate than they gain from a low standing charge [S56].

Tariff-first case

Competitive flat tariff

Well-matched TOU tariff

An effective average of around 21.5p/kWh in this model would cost:

9,000 × £0.215 = £1,935/year

Saving: £288/year, again without a battery.

Smart meters and half-hourly readings support TOU and dynamic tariff analysis; customers can request half-hourly data [S31]. A daytime-friendly structure is especially relevant: E.ON Next Smart Saver has described super-off-peak 2am–5am, off-peak 5am–4pm, peak 4pm–7pm and off-peak 7pm–2am [S48] [S57].

Implication for the home office: cheap daytime electricity may strengthen the case for switching tariff while weakening the case for battery storage.

Battery arbitrage model

What the battery itself adds

The relevant comparison is not against the current flat tariff; it is against the same suitable tariff without a battery. Under the illustrative model, the battery itself adds around £186/year for 5 kWh or £322/year for 10 kWh.

5 kWh model

  • 5.0 kWh nominal; 4.5 kWh usable
  • 90% AC round-trip efficiency
  • 4.05 kWh delivered per full useful cycle
  • 300 useful cycles/year
  • 10% average ten-year degradation allowance
  • 1,094 kWh/year average delivered energy
  • £4,250 assumed installed cost

Market context includes an Enphase 5 kWh system at about £4,100–£4,600 installed, 3.2 kW rated output and a stated 15-year product warranty [S68]. Wider indicative installed ranges are about £3,000–£5,500 [S10] [S53].

10 kWh model

  • 10.0 kWh nominal; 9.0 kWh initial usable
  • 90% AC round-trip efficiency
  • 8.10 kWh delivered per complete useful cycle
  • 260 useful cycles/year
  • 10% average ten-year degradation allowance
  • 1,895 kWh/year average delivered energy
  • £6,500 assumed installed cost

An AlphaESS illustration has 10.01 kWh nominal, 9.59 kWh usable, 5 kW maximum charge/discharge and a ten-year warranty; indicative installed pricing is £5,850–£6,850 [S68]. Wider ranges are around £5,000–£8,500 [S10] [S41].

Illustrative arbitrage calculation

Assume charging at 9p/kWh, avoiding imports at 27p/kWh, and 90% round-trip efficiency.

Delivered energy cost = 9p ÷ 0.90 = 10p/kWh

Gross saving = 27p − 10p = 17p/kWh delivered

Break-even hurdle

To repay £4,250 over ten years at 1,094 kWh/year, the 5 kWh system needs 38.8p/kWh battery-arbitrage value. With 9p charging, that means avoiding imports at nearly 48.8p/kWh on average.

The 10 kWh system needs 34.3p/kWh of benefit per delivered kWh; with 9p charging, avoided imports need to average about 44.3p/kWh.

Such prices can occur on dynamic tariffs, but the difficult requirement is avoiding them reliably, at sufficient volume, for a decade.

Risks and practical constraints

Storage is more than a kWh number

Degradation, warranty and repairs

The model already allows for a 10% average output reduction over ten years. At 20%, estimated annual arbitrage saving falls from around £186 to £165 for 5 kWh, and from £322 to around £286 for 10 kWh.

A £1,500 inverter replacement directly worsens a ten-year result by £1,500. Consumer and forum evidence supports allowing for around 10–15% storage losses and longer-term decline, but the quoted product warranty is decisive [S64].

Capacity is not power

kWh measures energy; kW measures power. A 10 kWh battery with a 3 kW inverter cannot supply more than 3 kW continuously. Coincident cooking, laundry, kettle, dishwasher, immersion or other loads can exceed that limit, leaving the home importing at peak price while the battery is discharging.

For a three-hour 4pm–7pm peak, a 10 kWh battery needs around 3.3 kW continuous discharge to empty within that window, unless it can also displace later high-price demand.

VAT, grants and export

Qualifying residential standalone batteries supplied and installed before 1 April 2027 can receive 0% VAT, including grid-charged batteries [S21] [S22]. This is not a grant; confirm treatment and total installed price in writing.

No general England-wide standalone-battery grant is assumed. Policy discussion of finance is not household-specific support [S23]. SEG income is also excluded: SEG concerns exported eligible renewable generation, not grid electricity stored in a standalone battery [S61].

Backup power has separate value

Outage resilience requires an islanding / backup design, commonly a backup gateway, critical-load consumer unit or dedicated circuits. Some installer examples put backup additions around £400–£700 [S53].

Ask the installer to confirm DNO requirements, G98 notification or G99 approval, export limitation and any consumer-unit upgrade [S11]. Backup may make a battery personally worthwhile, but it should be valued separately from bill savings.

Decision checklist

Obtain this evidence before committing

1. Half-hourly consumption data — at least 12 months

  • Weekday versus weekend demand
  • Winter versus summer demand
  • 00:00–05:00 overnight usage
  • 05:00–16:00 office usage
  • 16:00–19:00 peak-period usage
  • 19:00–00:00 evening usage
  • Highest half-hourly and hourly demand
  • Normal weekday imports during each supplier’s costly window

2. Written tariff information

  • Unit rate in every time band and daily standing charge
  • Duration, exit fees and eligibility conditions
  • Whether grid charging and battery automation are permitted
  • Whether rates can change and any dynamic price cap
  • Whether battery export is allowed or restricted

Tracker-style tariffs can carry price ceilings as high as 100p/kWh, making risk tolerance important for a 9,000 kWh importer [S17].

3. Three itemised battery quotations

  • Installed total including stated VAT treatment
  • Nominal and usable capacity; continuous and peak kW
  • AC round-trip efficiency and standby consumption
  • Warranty years, retained capacity and throughput limits
  • Inverter warranty, backup scope and DNO work
  • Consumer-unit upgrades, subscriptions and making-good
  • Likely post-warranty replacement cost

Best-for verdicts

Which path fits which priority?

Lowest risk

Competitive flat tariff. Predictable pricing and no capital investment.

Best financial savings

Tariff without battery

Well-matched TOU, Tracker or flat tariff. Around £150–£290/year in this model without hardware risk.

Backup power

Carefully designed 5 kWh battery. Only if resilience is worth paying for separately.

Very high peak loads

10 kWh battery. Only where interval data proves roughly 6–8 kWh/day of regular high-price demand and adequate inverter power.

Best choice for this household: trial a suitable tariff first, then reassess against real half-hourly data and firm battery-compatible terms.
