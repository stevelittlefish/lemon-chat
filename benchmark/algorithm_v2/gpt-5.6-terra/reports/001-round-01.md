## Updated assessment — England, 14 July 2026

### Bottom line

For this high-use, daytime-working household, **changing to a suitable time-of-use (ToU) tariff is likely worthwhile even without a battery**, provided the tariff is genuinely available to a non-EV, non-solar customer and its standing charge is not materially higher.

A battery charged overnight can reduce expensive daytime and early-evening imports, but on the evidence available it is **unlikely to repay its installed cost within ten years at 9,000 kWh/year**:

- A roughly **5 kWh battery** is borderline only under favourable pricing, high use during the 16:00–19:00 peak, low installed cost, and sustained tariff spreads. It does **not** beat tariff-switching alone in the central case.
- A roughly **10 kWh battery** is less attractive financially at 9,000 kWh/year because much of its stored electricity would displace ordinary daytime electricity rather than the highest-priced peak electricity. It becomes potentially plausible only at very high demand, unusually large price spreads, or a much lower installed price.
- The best expected financial outcome is therefore: **switch ToU tariff first, monitor half-hourly consumption, and defer the battery decision.**

This conclusion excludes backup-power value. Backup functionality can justify a battery for resilience, but it normally raises installation cost and should not be presented as a financial saving.

## 1. Tariff and technical evidence

A battery without solar creates value only by buying electricity when cheap and using it later instead of importing at a higher rate. It does not create electricity; round-trip losses mean more cheap electricity must be bought than is later delivered.

Illustrative current tariff evidence includes:

- EDF’s advertised EV tariffs offered **6.49–6.99p/kWh overnight from 23:00–06:00**, but eligibility for a battery-only household without an EV is not established by the supplier page and must not be assumed. The corresponding daytime rate and standing charge are also required before choosing it. [S5]
- One published EDF Empower Fixed example shows **10.361p/kWh from 01:00–04:00**, **20.22p/kWh normally**, and **30.22p/kWh from 16:00–19:00**. This is used below as an illustrative, non-guaranteed tariff structure—not as a confirmed household offer. [S4]
- Octopus Flux is **not suitable** for this household because it requires solar, battery, import and export arrangements. [S8]
- Octopus Go and Intelligent Go may offer attractive overnight rates, but published descriptions identify them principally as EV tariffs; Intelligent Go compatibility and eligibility must be checked. [S1]
- Government policy supports greater use of flexible tariffs, but Ofgem recognises that households with inflexible, time-specific demand may benefit less. This is important for a daytime home office. [S6]

The April 2026 reduction in electricity policy costs may lower unit rates, but wholesale, network and tariff-spread uncertainty remain material over a ten-year battery life. [S3]

## 2. Model assumptions

### Household load profile

Annual use: **9,000 kWh**, or **24.7 kWh/day**.

Because detailed half-hourly data are not yet available, the model assumes:

| Period | Share of annual use | Annual kWh | Rationale |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Cheap overnight window | 12% | 1,080 | Refrigeration, standby, some night use; little manual shifting assumed |
| Peak, 16:00–19:00 | 20% | 1,800 | Home-office/household evening overlap |
| Other daytime/evening | 68% | 6,120 | Predominantly daytime office and household demand |

The 12% overnight consumption is consumed **directly** at the off-peak rate. It is not incorrectly treated as battery-charged electricity.

### Tariffs and standing charge

| Assumption | Flat tariff | Illustrative ToU tariff |
|---|---:|---:|
| Unit rate | 24.70p/kWh | 10.361p off-peak; 20.22p normal; 30.22p peak |
| Standing charge | 55p/day assumed | 55p/day assumed |
| Annual standing charge | £201 | £201 |

Actual regional standing charges and tariff rates could change the outcome significantly. Obtain a postcode-specific tariff label before acting.

### Battery assumptions

Indicative market benchmarks, requiring confirmation through quotations:

| Item | “5 kWh” system | “10 kWh” system |
|---|---:|---:|
| Nominal capacity | about 5.2 kWh | about 9.5 kWh |
| Usable capacity | 4.68 kWh | 8.55 kWh |
| Continuous discharge power | about 2.6 kW | about 3.6 kW |
| Round-trip efficiency | 90% modelled | 90% modelled |
| Installed cost used | £4,050 | £6,150 |
| Warranty/degradation assumption | 10 years; 80% remaining usable capacity at year 10 | Same |
| Standby consumption | 10 W, about 88 kWh/year | 10 W, about 88 kWh/year |

Published indicative figures suggest roughly £3,800–£4,300 installed for a 5.2 kWh/4.68 kWh usable system and £5,800–£6,500 for a 9.5 kWh/8.55 kWh usable system, with output limits of about 2.6 kW and 3.6 kW respectively. [S10] A separate source gives an installed price around £4,000 for a 5.12 kWh system, but prices vary materially with inverter, backup hardware and installation complexity. [S9]

A 2.6 kW battery cannot cover simultaneous household loads above 2.6 kW; imports above that level remain charged at the live tariff rate. The same applies at 3.6 kW for the larger system. [S10]

### Cycle assumptions: deliberately not one full cycle daily

The household has enough total daily demand to use battery energy, but not necessarily enough **peak-period demand within the battery’s power limit** to complete a full useful cycle every day. The model therefore assumes:

| Battery | Delivered battery energy/year | Equivalent full cycles/year | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 5 kWh | 1,400 kWh | about 299 | About 3.8 kWh/day on average |
| 10 kWh | 1,580 kWh | about 185 | About 4.3 kWh/day on average |

The 10 kWh system is not assumed to cycle fully: its capacity exceeds typical peak-period demand, so a substantial part would displace cheaper normal-rate consumption.

Battery energy is assumed to displace:

- **5 kWh battery:** 75% peak imports and 25% normal-rate imports.
- **10 kWh battery:** 55% peak imports and 45% normal-rate imports.

These assumptions should be replaced with actual half-hourly smart-meter data.

## 3. Indicative annual cost comparison: 9,000 kWh/year

| Option | Annual electricity and standing-charge cost | Saving vs current flat tariff | Incremental saving vs ToU alone |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1. Competitive flat tariff, no battery | **£2,424** | — | — |
| 2. Illustrative ToU tariff, no battery | **£2,094** | **£330/year** | — |
| 3. ToU plus 5 kWh battery | **£1,888** | **£536/year** | **£206/year** |
| 4. ToU plus 10 kWh battery | **£1,887** | **£537/year** | **£207/year** |

The near-identical battery results are meaningful: the larger battery does not produce much more value in the central case because it increasingly displaces 20.22p/kWh normal-rate electricity rather than 30.22p/kWh peak electricity.

### Battery-arbitrage calculation

For the 5 kWh system:

- Average avoided import price:  
  75% × 30.22p + 25% × 20.22p = **27.72p/kWh**
- Cheap electricity required per delivered kWh at 90% efficiency:  
  1 ÷ 0.90 = **1.111 kWh imported**
- Effective storage energy cost:  
  10.361p ÷ 0.90 = **11.51p/kWh delivered**
- Gross battery margin: about **16.21p/kWh delivered**
- 1,400 kWh delivered × 16.21p = **£227/year**
- Less estimated standby energy cost: about **£18/year**
- Net first-year battery benefit: about **£209/year**.

The 10 kWh system has a lower average avoided-import price because more discharge occurs outside the peak period. Its greater throughput broadly offsets that disadvantage, but not enough to justify its higher capital cost.

## 4. Payback and ten-year outcome

The following uses constant real tariff prices, no financing cost, no repair cost, and linear degradation to 80% capacity in year ten. Battery replacement is not assumed within ten years, but neither is it guaranteed unnecessary after warranty expiry.

| Option | Upfront cost | Simple payback using battery-only saving | Ten-year net outcome vs remaining flat |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1. Flat tariff | £0 | n/a | £0 |
| 2. ToU, no battery | £0 | Immediate | **about +£3,300** |
| 3. 5 kWh battery + ToU | £4,050 | about **19–20 years** | **about +£1,100** |
| 4. 10 kWh battery + ToU | £6,150 | about **29–30 years** | **about –£1,000** |

The battery “simple payback” must be measured against **ToU without a battery**, not against the existing flat tariff. Crediting the battery with tariff-switching savings would overstate its economics.

Thus, over ten years:

- The **5 kWh battery may still leave the household slightly better off than doing nothing**, but it is around **£2,200 worse than simply moving to the ToU tariff without buying a battery**.
- The **10 kWh battery is worse than both the flat and ToU-only alternatives** in the central case.

No general UK grant is assumed for a battery-only installation. VAT treatment, DNO notification/connection requirements, export limitations and any tariff restrictions on grid charging/exporting should be confirmed with the installer and supplier. Solar export tariffs and SEG revenue do not apply without generation.

## 5. Usage sensitivity

| Annual use | ToU-only annual saving vs flat | 5 kWh battery annual incremental benefit vs ToU | 10 kWh battery annual incremental benefit vs ToU | Likely conclusion |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 6,000 kWh | ~£220 | ~£140 | ~£145 | Neither battery likely worthwhile |
| 9,000 kWh | ~£330 | ~£205 | ~£205 | ToU only is best |
| 11,000 kWh | ~£400 | ~£220 | ~£250 | 5 kWh becomes more plausible; 10 kWh remains marginal |

At lower use, the battery cannot be discharged sufficiently often or at sufficiently high-value periods. At 11,000 kWh/year, a 5 kWh battery can use more of its capacity during high-price hours; the 10 kWh system remains constrained by the limited three-hour peak window and its 3.6 kW output limit.

## 6. What would change the conclusion?

A battery becomes more attractive if several favourable factors occur together:

1. **Larger durable tariff spread.** The central case gives about 16p/kWh of gross arbitrage margin for the 5 kWh battery. To repay a £4,050 system over ten years from arbitrage alone would require roughly double the present annual battery value, or much more frequent/high-value discharge.
2. **Lower installed price.** A 5 kWh all-in quote nearer £2,500–£3,000 would materially improve the case. A £6,000-plus 10 kWh system is difficult to justify without solar or backup value.
3. **High actual 16:00–19:00 demand.** Regular peak demand within the inverter’s output limit is more valuable than general daytime consumption.
4. **A tariff genuinely offering cheap overnight power and expensive peak power to a non-EV customer.**
5. **More than ten years of useful battery life**, low standby consumption, low degradation and no costly inverter/battery repair.
6. **Valuable backup power.** This is a non-financial benefit, and backup hardware may increase the upfront price.

Conversely, the case worsens sharply if off-peak prices rise, peak spreads narrow, the supplier withdraws the tariff, the battery is unable to charge/discharge automatically, the home’s peak demand exceeds battery power, or degradation/replacement occurs earlier than assumed. Flexible tariff rates are not guaranteed for ten years. [S1][S8]

## Recommendation

1. **Switch tariff first**, subject to checking eligibility, regional rates, standing charge, exit fees and whether the tariff is available without an EV. The illustrative ToU saving of roughly **£330/year** is meaningful and requires no capital expenditure.
2. Collect at least **12 months of half-hourly smart-meter data** after the tariff change.
3. Consider a **5 kWh battery only** if the data show repeated, substantial 16:00–19:00 imports; an all-in quote is low; and the expected battery-only saving exceeds roughly £350–£400/year.
4. Do **not** choose a 10 kWh battery primarily for arbitrage at 9,000 kWh/year unless load data show much greater evening/peak demand than assumed or the system is being bought partly for resilience.

### Strongest argument against this recommendation

The strongest counterargument is that future smart tariffs could offer much wider and more frequent price spreads than the central model, while the household’s high daytime office load provides a dependable sink for stored overnight electricity. If those conditions persist, a battery could earn more than modelled. However, that is tariff speculation rather than a bankable ten-year saving: the cited suppliers describe flexible or variable tariff arrangements, and EV/solar tariff eligibility cannot be presumed. [S1][S5][S8]

### Data and quotations to obtain before purchase

- Half-hourly import data for at least 12 months, especially 16:00–19:00 demand and overnight baseline demand.
- Postcode-specific tariff quotes showing all unit rates, time windows, standing charge, contract term, exit fee, eligibility and export/grid-charging restrictions.
- Confirmation that a non-EV, battery-only household can use the proposed tariff.
- At least three itemised battery quotations showing usable kWh, continuous/peak charge and discharge power, inverter rating, backup capability, installation works, VAT, DNO process and monitoring/control hardware.
- Manufacturer warranty terms: years, throughput cap, minimum retained capacity, labour cover, inverter cover and exclusions for tariff-arbitrage cycling.
- Written estimates of standby consumption, expected degradation, post-warranty repair cost and battery/inverter replacement cost.
- Confirmation that automated charging/discharging can respond to the selected tariff without discharging during cheap periods or charging during expensive ones.
