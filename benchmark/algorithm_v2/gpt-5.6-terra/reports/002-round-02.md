## Updated assessment — England, 14 July 2026

### Bottom line

For this 9,000 kWh/year, high-daytime-use household, the best financial action is likely to be:

1. **Investigate and switch to a suitable smart time-of-use (ToU) tariff first;**
2. **Use 6–12 months of half-hourly data to test the real opportunity;**
3. **Defer a battery unless a low installed-price quote and unusually favourable tariff/load evidence emerge.**

A battery without solar can save money by charging cheaply overnight and discharging during more expensive periods. However, it does not create electricity, loses roughly 10% in storage, consumes standby power, degrades, and exposes the household to future tariff uncertainty.

At the central assumptions:

- **ToU without a battery:** saves about **£330/year** versus the present flat tariff.
- **5 kWh battery plus ToU:** adds only about **£205/year** of first-year saving versus ToU alone; simple payback is roughly **19–20 years**.
- **10 kWh battery plus ToU:** adds a similar annual benefit but costs much more; simple payback is roughly **29–30 years**.

Therefore:

- A **5 kWh battery is not normally financially worthwhile within ten years** at an installed cost around £4,050.
- A **10 kWh battery is less attractive** for arbitrage alone at this demand profile.
- A **ToU tariff without a battery is the strongest financial option** in the central case.

Backup power may justify a battery for resilience, but backup hardware can increase cost and should be valued separately from bill savings.

## 1. Tariff evidence and suitability

The current flat rate of **24.7p/kWh** is already relatively competitive. The July–September 2026 average capped-variable electricity benchmark is about **26.11p/kWh plus 57.19p/day**, including VAT, although regional rates vary. [S17] At 9,000 kWh/year, paying 1.41p/kWh below that benchmark is worth about £127/year before standing-charge differences.

A smart meter permits access to ToU tariffs, but tariff choice matters more than the label:

- EDF advertises smart tariffs with seven-hour overnight windows, including EV off-peak prices around **6.49–6.99p/kWh** as at April 2026. Eligibility of battery-only, non-EV households must be confirmed in writing. [S11]
- E.ON Next’s standard Next Drive advertises **9p/kWh from midnight to 06:00**, but is marketed around EV ownership; its Smart version’s 8p/kWh price requires an eligible EV and compatible charger. [S18]
- E.ON Next also advertises Smart Saver structures with cheaper periods extending through parts of the daytime, potentially well suited to a home-office load. However, rates, postcode availability and eligibility must be confirmed. [S16]
- Economy 7/Economy 10 can be worse than a flat tariff if daytime use is high and the cheap-rate share is small. [S12]
- Octopus Flux is not appropriate: it is designed around solar generation, export and battery arrangements. [S8]

The model below uses a published EDF Empower-style example—10.361p/kWh from 01:00–04:00, 20.22p normally and 30.22p from 16:00–19:00—as an **illustrative tariff shape**, not as a guaranteed available offer. [S4]

## 2. Assumptions

### Household demand profile

Annual consumption is 9,000 kWh, or 24.7 kWh/day.

Pending real half-hourly data, the model assumes:

| Period | Share | Annual use | Comment |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Cheap overnight period | 12% | 1,080 kWh | Direct overnight household use, not battery charging |
| Peak, 16:00–19:00 | 20% | 1,800 kWh | Evening and late-working demand |
| Other daytime/evening | 68% | 6,120 kWh | Mainly office and household use |

This household’s high daytime demand is helpful for using stored energy, but not all daytime electricity is high-value electricity. The key question is how much demand occurs in the 16:00–19:00 peak window and remains below the battery/inverter output limit.

### Tariff and standing-charge assumptions

| Item | Flat tariff | Illustrative ToU tariff |
|---|---:|---:|
| Unit price | 24.70p/kWh | 10.361p off-peak; 20.22p normal; 30.22p peak |
| Standing charge | 55p/day | 55p/day |
| Annual standing charge | £201 | £201 |

Actual regional standing charges, time windows and unit rates could materially alter the results.

### Battery assumptions

| Item | “5 kWh” system | “10 kWh” system |
|---|---:|---:|
| Nominal capacity | 5.2 kWh | 9.5 kWh |
| Usable capacity | 4.68 kWh | 8.55 kWh |
| Continuous discharge power | 2.6 kW | 3.6 kW |
| Round-trip efficiency | 90% | 90% |
| Installed cost modelled | £4,050 | £6,150 |
| Standby consumption | 10 W; 88 kWh/year | 10 W; 88 kWh/year |
| Warranty/degradation model | 10 years; 80% retained capacity in year 10 | Same |

Indicative installed-price evidence suggests roughly £3,800–£4,300 for a 5.2 kWh system and £5,800–£6,500 for a 9.5 kWh system, depending on inverter, electrical work and backup hardware. [S9][S10] Quotes should state whether VAT is included and whether zero-rated VAT treatment for qualifying battery installation has been applied.

The model does **not** assume a full cycle every day:

| Battery | Delivered energy/year | Equivalent full cycles/year | Peak/normal displacement |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 5 kWh | 1,400 kWh | 299 | 75% peak / 25% normal |
| 10 kWh | 1,580 kWh | 185 | 55% peak / 45% normal |

The larger battery is under-utilised because the three-hour peak period and 3.6 kW discharge limit restrict how much high-price electricity it can replace.

## 3. Annual cost comparison: 9,000 kWh/year

| Option | Annual cost including standing charge | Saving vs flat | Extra saving vs ToU only |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1. Flat tariff, no battery | **£2,424** | — | — |
| 2. ToU tariff, no battery | **£2,094** | **£330** | — |
| 3. ToU plus 5 kWh battery | **£1,888** | **£536** | **£206** |
| 4. ToU plus 10 kWh battery | **£1,887** | **£537** | **£207** |

For the 5 kWh system, the average avoided import price is:

- 75% × 30.22p + 25% × 20.22p = **27.72p/kWh**.

At 90% round-trip efficiency, each delivered kWh needs 1.111 kWh of off-peak import:

- Delivered storage cost: 10.361p ÷ 0.90 = **11.51p/kWh**.
- Gross arbitrage margin: 27.72p − 11.51p = **16.21p/kWh delivered**.
- Gross annual saving: 1,400 kWh × 16.21p = **£227**.
- Less standby electricity: roughly **£18/year**.
- Net first-year battery benefit: approximately **£209/year**.

The 10 kWh battery moves more energy but replaces more 20.22p/kWh normal-rate electricity. That is why its annual bill outcome is almost identical despite its much larger capital cost.

## 4. Payback and ten-year outcome

Constant real tariff prices are assumed. No financing cost, repair cost, replacement cost or resale value is included. Capacity declines linearly to 80% by year ten.

| Option | Upfront cost | Battery-only simple payback | Ten-year outcome vs flat tariff |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Flat tariff | £0 | n/a | £0 |
| ToU only | £0 | Immediate | **about +£3,300** |
| 5 kWh battery + ToU | £4,050 | **19–20 years** | **about +£1,100** |
| 10 kWh battery + ToU | £6,150 | **29–30 years** | **about –£1,000** |

The relevant comparison is battery versus **ToU without battery**, not battery versus the current flat tariff.

On that basis, after degradation:

- The 5 kWh battery provides about **£1,900** of ten-year battery-arbitrage benefit, versus an assumed £4,050 cost.
- The 10 kWh battery provides about **£1,860** of ten-year arbitrage benefit, versus £6,150 cost.

Neither beats ToU alone in the central case.

## 5. Sensitivity

| Annual usage | ToU-only saving vs flat | 5 kWh battery extra saving vs ToU | 10 kWh battery extra saving vs ToU |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 6,000 kWh | ~£220/year | ~£140/year | ~£145/year |
| 9,000 kWh | ~£330/year | ~£205/year | ~£205/year |
| 11,000 kWh | ~£400/year | ~£220/year | ~£250/year |

Other important sensitivities:

- **Peak share:** for the 5 kWh battery, changing the displaced load from 75% to 50% peak reduces first-year saving by roughly £35; 100% peak displacement adds roughly £35, assuming throughput remains unchanged.
- **Tariff spread:** if off-peak power were 9p/kWh and the displaced electricity averaged 35p/kWh, the 5 kWh battery’s annual benefit could approach £330–£350 before degradation. Sustained spreads of this size are uncertain.
- **Installed cost:** at central tariff assumptions, a 5 kWh system would need to cost about **£1,900 installed** to beat ToU-only over ten years. A £2,500–£3,000 quote improves the case but still needs better spreads or more peak discharge.
- **Degradation/repairs:** faster degradation, inverter failure, higher standby use or a replacement requirement makes the battery materially worse. Warranties often have throughput caps and exclusions that must be checked.

There is no general battery-only grant assumed. The installer should handle DNO notification or approval, normally under G98/G99 arrangements as applicable. The supplier must confirm whether grid charging, automated control, or export from a grid-charged battery is restricted. SEG export income is irrelevant without generation.

## Recommendation and evidence to obtain

**Recommendation:** move to a suitable ToU tariff first, especially one with low daytime as well as overnight rates. E.ON’s Smart Saver-style structure may be particularly relevant to a daytime home-office load, but only a postcode-specific quote can establish this. [S16] Do not assume EV tariffs are available without an EV. [S11][S18]

Consider a 5 kWh battery only if all of the following are true:

- half-hourly data show sustained imports during 16:00–19:00;
- the battery can cover those loads within its power limit;
- a suitable tariff is contractually available;
- installed price is unusually low; and
- expected battery-only saving is at least about **£450/year** for a £4,050 system, or the price is much lower.

Do not select a 10 kWh battery primarily for tariff arbitrage at 9,000 kWh/year.

**Strongest argument against this recommendation:** future flexible tariffs may have wider and more frequent price spreads, while this household’s high daytime demand gives stored electricity a reliable use. That could improve battery returns. But it is speculative: tariff eligibility, prices and time windows are not bankable for ten years. [S1][S5][S8]

Before buying, obtain:

- 12 months of half-hourly import data, especially 16:00–19:00 and overnight use;
- postcode-specific tariff labels, standing charges, eligibility rules, exit fees and grid-charging/export restrictions;
- three itemised battery quotes covering usable capacity, charge/discharge power, inverter, backup capability, VAT, DNO work and monitoring;
- warranty details: capacity retention, throughput cap, labour cover, inverter cover and arbitrage-cycling exclusions;
- written estimates of standby use, degradation and post-warranty repair or replacement costs.
