## Updated assessment — England, 14 July 2026

### Bottom line

For this high-use household (about 9,000 kWh/year), **changing tariff is likely to be financially worthwhile; installing a battery without solar panels usually is not**.

The preferred sequence is:

1. Download and analyse 12 months of half-hourly smart-meter data;
2. Compare postcode-specific fixed, variable and time-of-use (ToU) tariffs;
3. Switch to a suitable ToU tariff without a battery;
4. Consider a battery only if the data show substantial 16:00–19:00 imports, a battery-eligible tariff is available on favourable terms, and an unusually low installed quotation is obtained.

Under the central assumptions:

- A suitable ToU tariff without storage saves about **£330/year** against the current 24.7p/kWh flat tariff.
- A roughly 5 kWh battery adds about **£205/year** of first-year savings beyond the ToU tariff alone.
- A roughly 10 kWh battery adds about **£220/year** in this model, but costs much more and is less well utilised.
- Neither battery is likely to repay its installed cost within ten years through import-price arbitrage alone.

High daytime home-office consumption is helpful only where it coincides with expensive tariff periods or allows reliable discharge. It does **not** by itself justify a battery: the crucial period is likely to be the roughly 16:00–19:00 peak, not general daytime use.

## 1. Tariffs and eligibility

The present flat rate of 24.7p/kWh is competitive relative to the July–September 2026 Ofgem capped-variable benchmark of roughly 26.11p/kWh plus a 57.19p/day standing charge, although regional rates differ. [S17] A competitive fixed tariff should therefore remain part of the comparison rather than assuming the price cap is unavoidable. E.ON Next, for example, notes that fixed and tracker products may differ materially from the cap, although quoted average-customer savings cannot be applied directly to this unusually electricity-intensive household. [S32]

A smart meter and half-hourly settlement are normally required for smart ToU tariffs. EDF and E.ON both identify smart-meter data as necessary for their relevant smart products. [S20][S28][S31] The household already meets an important practical condition.

However, advertised cheap overnight EV rates should not be assumed available:

- Octopus Go and Intelligent Octopus Go commonly require a qualifying EV or plug-in hybrid. [S21]
- EDF’s advertised 6.49–6.99p/kWh EV rates are similarly not safe assumptions for a battery-only household without written supplier confirmation. [S11][S20][S22]
- E.ON Next Drive’s illustrative 8–9p/kWh overnight rates are EV-oriented and should not be used in the battery case unless eligibility is confirmed. [S31]

Potentially relevant products include EDF Empower Fixed, which EDF says can accept customers with “a battery,” requires half-hourly smart-meter readings and has no exit fee. Its published material refers to an overnight charging discount, but does not provide sufficiently stable or complete regional rates to use as a ten-year forecast. [S27] EDF’s newer three-rate structure also limits the expensive period to 16:00–19:00, with cheaper overnight electricity and intermediate daytime/evening pricing. This could suit a home-office household even without a battery because much working-day use may fall outside the evening peak. [S30]

The calculation below therefore uses an illustrative published EDF-style three-rate tariff, not a guaranteed offer:

| Period | Assumed rate |
|---|---:|
| Off-peak, 01:00–04:00 | 10.361p/kWh |
| Normal period | 20.22p/kWh |
| Peak, 16:00–19:00 | 30.22p/kWh |
| Standing charge | 55p/day |

Actual postcode rates, hours, exit fees, eligibility, automated-control conditions and tariff availability must be checked before purchase.

## 2. Load profile and battery assumptions

Annual use is 9,000 kWh, or 24.7 kWh/day. In the absence of actual half-hourly data, the indicative profile is:

| Period | Share | Annual consumption |
|---|---:|---:|
| Overnight off-peak | 12% | 1,080 kWh |
| Peak, 16:00–19:00 | 20% | 1,800 kWh |
| Other daytime/evening | 68% | 6,120 kWh |

The overnight use is assumed to be consumed directly, for example by refrigeration, broadband/network equipment and background household load. It is **not** assumed to be charged through the battery.

| Assumption | Roughly 5 kWh system | Roughly 10 kWh system |
|---|---:|---:|
| Nominal capacity | 5.2 kWh | 9.5 kWh |
| Usable capacity assumed | 4.68 kWh | 8.55 kWh |
| Continuous discharge power | 2.6 kW | 3.6 kW |
| Round-trip efficiency | 90% | 90% |
| Installed cost assumed | £4,050 | £6,150 |
| Standby consumption | 88 kWh/year | 88 kWh/year |
| Capacity in year ten | 80% of original usable capacity | 80% |

Indicative installed-price evidence supports approximately £3,800–£4,300 for a smaller system and £5,800–£6,500 for a roughly 10 kWh system, depending on inverter, backup provision and electrical work. [S9][S10] Supplier and manufacturer claims of £800–£1,050 annual savings or five-year payback should not be relied upon: the published examples commonly include solar generation, export revenue, EV charging, unspecified cycling assumptions or incomplete equipment details. [S25][S27][S29]

The model does not assume a full cycle every day:

| Battery | Delivered energy/year | Equivalent full cycles/year | Assumed displaced imports |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 5 kWh | 1,400 kWh | about 299 | 75% peak, 25% normal |
| 10 kWh | 1,580 kWh | about 185 | 55% peak, 45% normal |

The 10 kWh battery is deliberately under-utilised. Its additional capacity is less likely to be needed during the three-hour peak every day. Although a 3.6 kW inverter could theoretically deliver 10.8 kWh over three hours, useful output depends on simultaneous household demand, state of charge, weather-independent seasonal load patterns and tariff controls.

## 3. Indicative annual electricity costs: 9,000 kWh/year

| Option | Annual electricity cost including standing charge | Saving vs flat tariff | Extra saving vs ToU-only |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1. Flat tariff, no battery | **£2,424** | — | — |
| 2. ToU tariff, no battery | **£2,094** | **£330** | — |
| 3. ToU plus 5 kWh battery | **about £1,888** | **about £536** | **about £205** |
| 4. ToU plus 10 kWh battery | **about £1,873** | **about £551** | **about £220** |

For the 5 kWh battery, the average displaced import price is:

- 75% × 30.22p + 25% × 20.22p = **27.72p/kWh**.

At 90% round-trip efficiency, one delivered kWh requires 1.111 kWh of off-peak electricity:

- Charging cost: 10.361p ÷ 0.90 = **11.51p/kWh delivered**;
- Gross arbitrage margin: 27.72p − 11.51p = **16.21p/kWh**;
- Gross annual saving: 1,400 kWh × 16.21p = about **£227**;
- Less standby electricity: about **£18/year**;
- Net first-year battery benefit: about **£209/year**, rounded to £205.

The 10 kWh system moves slightly more energy but substitutes more normal-rate electricity. Its estimated average displaced price is about 26.62p/kWh, producing a first-year net benefit of about £220 after charging losses and standby consumption. That modest improvement does not justify its much higher capital cost.

## 4. Payback and ten-year result

The ten-year calculation assumes constant real tariff prices, linear battery degradation to 80% capacity in year ten, no financing cost, no resale value and no major repair. These assumptions are favourable to batteries: inverter replacement, labour, failure, tariff withdrawal or worse degradation would reduce returns.

| Option | Upfront cost | Simple battery payback | Ten-year result vs staying on flat tariff |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Flat tariff | £0 | n/a | £0 |
| ToU only | £0 | Immediate | **about +£3,300** |
| 5 kWh battery plus ToU | £4,050 | **about 19–20 years** | **about +£1,100** |
| 10 kWh battery plus ToU | £6,150 | **about 28 years** | **about –£900** |

The better comparison is battery versus ToU-only. Over ten years, allowing for degradation:

- The 5 kWh battery produces about **£1,850–£1,900** of arbitrage benefit, below its £4,050 installed cost.
- The 10 kWh battery produces about **£1,950–£2,000**, well below its £6,150 cost.

No general battery-only grant has been assumed. VAT treatment should be confirmed in quotations, particularly for a standalone battery installation. DNO notification or approval will normally be required under G98 or G99 arrangements, depending on export capability and system characteristics. SEG income should not be included: SEG is intended for export from qualifying renewable generation, not electricity imported cheaply, stored and re-exported. [S26][S33] Any grid charging, export or automated-control arrangement must be explicitly permitted by the supplier’s tariff terms.

## 5. Sensitivity

| Annual consumption | ToU-only saving vs flat | 5 kWh extra saving vs ToU | 10 kWh extra saving vs ToU |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 6,000 kWh | about £220/year | about £140/year | about £150/year |
| 9,000 kWh | about £330/year | about £205/year | about £220/year |
| 11,000 kWh | about £400/year | about £220/year | about £250/year |

The battery conclusion changes only if several favourable conditions occur together:

- **Wider price spreads:** charging near 9p/kWh and reliably displacing imports near 35p/kWh could raise a 5 kWh battery’s benefit towards £330–£350/year before degradation.
- **More peak demand:** if nearly all battery output displaces 16:00–19:00 imports rather than normal-rate use, the 5 kWh benefit improves by roughly £70/year at the same annual throughput.
- **Lower installed price:** the 5 kWh system would need to cost roughly **£1,900–£2,200 installed** to compete with ToU-only over ten years under central tariff assumptions.
- **High utilisation within warranty limits:** more cycling improves savings, but increases degradation risk and may consume warranty throughput allowance.
- **Battery-compatible tariff certainty:** a long-lived, low overnight/high peak tariff arrangement would strengthen the case, but suppliers can change rates, windows and eligibility. [S21][S27][S30]

## Recommendation

**Best financial outcome:** switch to a suitable ToU tariff without buying a battery. It produces meaningful savings of roughly £330/year in the central case without capital risk.

A **5 kWh battery** is only potentially defensible if half-hourly data show sustained peak imports, the inverter can serve those loads, a battery-eligible tariff is contractually available, and the installed quotation is unusually low.

A **10 kWh battery** is not recommended for tariff arbitrage alone. It has more capacity than this profile can reliably use during expensive periods.

**Strongest argument against this recommendation:** future flexible tariffs may provide wider and more frequent spreads, while the household’s high office-related demand could provide dependable battery discharge. But tariff terms, smart-control arrangements, price spreads and supplier eligibility are not bankable for a ten-year investment. [S21][S29][S30]

Before purchase, obtain:

- Twelve months of half-hourly import data, particularly 16:00–19:00 and overnight;
- Postcode-specific tariff illustrations and written battery eligibility confirmation;
- Three itemised quotations including VAT, inverter, DNO work, backup hardware, monitoring and installation;
- Guaranteed usable capacity, continuous charge/discharge power, standby consumption and efficiency;
- Warranty retention terms, throughput limits, labour cover and inverter warranty;
- Written confirmation on grid charging, export restrictions and automated tariff control.
