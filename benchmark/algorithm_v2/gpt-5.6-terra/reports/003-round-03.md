## Updated assessment — England, 14 July 2026

### Bottom line

For this 9,000 kWh/year household, **switching tariff is likely to be worthwhile; buying a battery without solar usually is not**.

The financially preferable sequence is:

1. **Obtain half-hourly smart-meter data and compare available tariffs;**
2. **Switch to a suitable time-of-use (ToU) tariff without a battery;**
3. **Only then consider a battery if real peak-period imports, tariff terms and quotations are materially better than the central case.**

At the central assumptions:

- A suitable ToU tariff without a battery saves about **£330/year** versus the current 24.7p/kWh flat tariff.
- A roughly 5 kWh battery adds only about **£205/year** first-year saving beyond ToU alone.
- A roughly 10 kWh battery adds about **£190/year** in the conservative utilisation case, despite costing substantially more.
- Neither battery is likely to recover its cost within ten years through tariff arbitrage alone.

The household’s high daytime office demand makes it more likely that stored electricity can be used, but this is not automatically valuable: savings depend mainly on demand during expensive tariff periods, especially roughly 16:00–19:00, rather than daytime demand generally.

## 1. Tariffs and tariff eligibility

The present flat rate of **24.7p/kWh** is relatively competitive against the July–September 2026 capped-variable benchmark of about **26.11p/kWh plus 57.19p/day**, although regional rates differ. [S17] At 9,000 kWh/year, the household already pays roughly £127/year less for electricity than at that benchmark, before standing-charge differences.

A smart meter and half-hourly readings are essential for most smart tariffs. EDF’s April 2026 EV tariff announcement states that relevant tariffs require a smart meter and half-hourly readings, with seven-hour overnight windows such as 23:00–06:00. [S20][S22] However, EDF’s advertised 6.49–6.99p/kWh overnight rates are EV-oriented offers; **a battery-only household must obtain written confirmation of eligibility before relying on them**. [S11][S20][S22]

Similarly, Octopus Go and Intelligent Octopus Go should not be assumed available without a qualifying EV or plug-in hybrid. [S21] Octopus Intelligent Flux may be relevant for an approved battery, but tariff conditions, import rates, export treatment and whether a non-solar installation is accepted must be checked. Smart-tariff terms also warn that prices, availability and technology arrangements can change; historic price outcomes are not a ten-year guarantee. [S21]

Economy 7-style tariffs can be worse than a flat tariff where daytime consumption is high and the cheap-rate share is low. [S12] The most promising tariff for this household may therefore be one with both a low overnight rate and competitively priced daytime periods, rather than an EV-only tariff with an expensive daytime rate. E.ON Next Smart Saver-type products may be worth checking locally. [S16]

The calculations below use an illustrative EDF Empower-style tariff shape, not a guaranteed offer:

| Period | Rate |
|---|---:|
| 01:00–04:00 off-peak | 10.361p/kWh |
| Normal | 20.22p/kWh |
| Peak, 16:00–19:00 | 30.22p/kWh |
| Standing charge | 55p/day |

This tariff example is based on published EDF material. [S4] Actual postcode-specific rates, time windows, standing charges, exit fees and eligibility could change the conclusion.

## 2. Household-load and battery assumptions

Annual use is 9,000 kWh, or 24.7 kWh/day. Pending actual half-hourly data, the assumed profile is:

| Period | Share of annual use | Annual use |
|---|---:|---:|
| Overnight off-peak | 12% | 1,080 kWh |
| Peak, 16:00–19:00 | 20% | 1,800 kWh |
| Other daytime/evening | 68% | 6,120 kWh |

The 1,080 kWh overnight amount is assumed to be directly consumed electricity, such as refrigeration, network equipment and some household demand. It is **not** assumed that all overnight consumption goes through the battery.

Illustrative battery systems are modelled as follows:

| Item | Roughly 5 kWh battery | Roughly 10 kWh battery |
|---|---:|---:|
| Nominal capacity | 5.2 kWh | 9.5 kWh |
| Usable capacity assumed | 4.68 kWh | 8.55 kWh |
| Continuous discharge power | 2.6 kW | 3.6 kW |
| Round-trip efficiency | 90% | 90% |
| Installed cost assumed | £4,050 | £6,150 |
| Standby electricity | 88 kWh/year |
| Capacity in year 10 | 80% of initial usable capacity |

Indicative installed-price evidence supports approximately £3,800–£4,300 for a small system and £5,800–£6,500 for a roughly 10 kWh system, depending on inverter, backup arrangement and electrical work. [S9][S10] Manufacturer claims of £1,050 annual savings and five-year battery-only payback should be treated as marketing rather than an applicable forecast: the cited material does not state capacity, tariff rates, cycling, losses, standby load or warranty assumptions. [S25] Likewise, generic product pages do not provide sufficient usable-capacity, power or warranty data for modelling a specific Fox ESS system. [S24] A text-accessible GivEnergy brochure would need review before it can support equipment-specific claims. [S23]

The model deliberately does not assume one full cycle every day:

| Battery | Delivered energy/year | Equivalent full cycles/year | Displaced demand |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 5 kWh | 1,400 kWh | about 299 | 75% peak / 25% normal |
| 10 kWh | 1,580 kWh | about 185 | 55% peak / 45% normal |

The 10 kWh system is under-used because its extra capacity is less likely to displace the highest-priced three-hour peak imports consistently. A 3.6 kW inverter can supply at most 10.8 kWh over three hours, but actual useful discharge is constrained by the household’s simultaneous load, battery state of charge, winter demand pattern and tariff schedule.

## 3. Indicative annual electricity costs: 9,000 kWh/year

| Option | Annual cost including standing charge | Saving versus flat tariff | Extra saving versus ToU only |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1. Flat tariff, no battery | **£2,424** | — | — |
| 2. ToU tariff, no battery | **£2,094** | **£330** | — |
| 3. ToU plus 5 kWh battery | **about £1,888** | **about £536** | **about £205** |
| 4. ToU plus 10 kWh battery | **about £1,903** | **about £521** | **about £190** |

For the 5 kWh system, the avoided import price is assumed to average:

- 75% × 30.22p + 25% × 20.22p = **27.72p/kWh delivered**.

At 90% round-trip efficiency, one delivered kWh requires 1.111 kWh of cheap-rate import:

- Stored-energy cost: 10.361p ÷ 0.90 = **11.51p/kWh delivered**.
- Gross arbitrage margin: 27.72p − 11.51p = **16.21p/kWh delivered**.
- Gross annual benefit: 1,400 kWh × 16.21p = about **£227**.
- Less estimated standby electricity: about **£18/year**.
- Net first-year battery benefit: about **£209/year**, rounded to £205 in the comparison table.

The 10 kWh system moves slightly more energy but, under the conservative profile, displaces proportionally more normal-rate electricity. Its larger capital cost is therefore not matched by proportionally higher savings.

## 4. Payback and ten-year financial outcome

The ten-year comparison assumes constant real tariff prices, linear capacity decline to 80% in year ten, no financing cost, no resale value and no major repair. That is favourable to the battery: inverter failure, replacement batteries, labour costs or a poorer tariff spread would worsen results.

| Option | Upfront cost | Battery-only simple payback | Ten-year result versus remaining on flat tariff |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Flat tariff | £0 | n/a | £0 |
| ToU only | £0 | Immediate | **about +£3,300** |
| 5 kWh battery + ToU | £4,050 | **about 19–20 years** | **about +£1,100** |
| 10 kWh battery + ToU | £6,150 | **about 32 years** | **about –£1,100** |

The important comparison is battery versus **ToU without battery**:

- Five-year/ten-year battery marketing claims are not supported by this household’s likely arbitrage throughput. [S25]
- The 5 kWh battery produces roughly **£1,900** of ten-year arbitrage benefit after degradation, against £4,050 upfront cost.
- The 10 kWh battery produces roughly **£1,700–£1,800**, against £6,150 upfront cost.

No general battery-only grant is assumed. VAT treatment should be confirmed in each quote, including whether zero-rated VAT for qualifying battery installations is applied. DNO notification or approval is normally required under G98/G99 arrangements, depending on system characteristics. Grid charging, export and automated-control rules must be confirmed with both installer and supplier. SEG income is generally irrelevant without generation.

## 5. Sensitivity

| Annual use | ToU-only saving versus flat | 5 kWh battery extra saving versus ToU | 10 kWh battery extra saving versus ToU |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 6,000 kWh | about £220/year | about £140/year | about £145/year |
| 9,000 kWh | about £330/year | about £205/year | about £190/year |
| 11,000 kWh | about £400/year | about £220/year | about £250/year |

The battery case improves materially only if several favourable factors combine:

- **Wider spreads:** 9p/kWh charging electricity and a 35p/kWh displaced import price could raise a 5 kWh battery benefit towards £330–£350/year before degradation.
- **More genuine peak demand:** moving from 50% to 100% peak displacement changes the 5 kWh result by roughly £70/year, assuming the same throughput.
- **Low installed cost:** at central assumptions, the 5 kWh system needs to cost roughly **£1,900 installed** to outperform ToU-only over ten years.
- **High utilisation without excessive degradation:** more cycles increase savings but also use warranty throughput allowance and may accelerate degradation.

Conversely, faster capacity loss, inverter failure, higher standby use, tariff withdrawal, a smaller price spread, or inability to discharge during peak periods makes the investment worse.

## Recommendation

**Best financial outcome:** switch to a suitable ToU tariff without buying a battery. This produces meaningful savings with no capital risk.

A **5 kWh battery** is only potentially defensible if half-hourly data show sustained 16:00–19:00 imports, the battery can serve those loads within its power limit, a suitable battery-eligible tariff is contractually available, and an unusually low installed quote is obtained. A **10 kWh battery** is not recommended primarily for tariff arbitrage at this load profile.

**Strongest argument against this recommendation:** future flexible tariffs could offer wider, more frequent spreads, and the high daytime office load gives the household a reliable use for stored electricity. However, that outcome is uncertain: supplier eligibility, tariff schedules, price spreads and smart-control arrangements are not bankable for ten years. [S21]

Before purchase, obtain:

- 12 months of half-hourly import data, especially overnight and 16:00–19:00;
- postcode-specific tariff quotes and written eligibility confirmation;
- three itemised battery quotations including VAT, DNO work, inverter, backup hardware and monitoring;
- usable capacity, continuous charge/discharge power, standby consumption and efficiency figures;
- warranty capacity-retention terms, throughput caps, labour cover and inverter cover;
- written confirmation of grid-charging, export and automated-control restrictions.
