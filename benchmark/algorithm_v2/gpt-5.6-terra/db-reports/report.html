Is a home battery worthwhile without solar panels?

For a 9,000 kWh/year home with substantial daytime office demand and no planned solar installation, the strongest financial move is likely to be changing electricity tariff before buying a battery.

Bottom line

Switch tariff first. Do not buy a battery for arbitrage alone.

A suitable time-of-use (ToU) tariff could save about £330/year with no equipment cost. A roughly 5 kWh battery adds only about £205–£210 in year one beyond that tariff saving, against an indicative installed cost of £4,050.

Recommended sequence

  1. Get 12 months of half-hourly smart-meter data.
  2. Compare postcode-specific flat and ToU quotes.
  3. Switch tariff without a battery.
  4. Consider a small battery only if the data and quotation are unusually favourable.

Why tariff comes first: this household’s high office load matters less than when that load occurs. The battery opportunity is concentrated in the 16:00–19:00 expensive period; much working-day use may fall outside it. Assessment basis

The model concerns an English household using approximately 9,000 kWh/year, or 24.7 kWh/day. It deliberately excludes EV-only rates, solar generation, export revenue, grants, financing costs and major repair costs—assumptions that make the battery case more, not less, favourable.

Flat-rate baseline

The existing 24.7p/kWh unit rate is relatively competitive against the July–September 2026 national-average capped-variable benchmark of about 26.11p/kWh plus a 57.19p/day standing charge. Regional rates still differ. [S17]

9,000 kWh × £0.247 + £201 standing charge ≈ £2,424/year

A smart meter is important: relevant ToU products normally require half-hourly data. [S20] [S28]

Illustrative three-rate tariff

The model uses an EDF-style structure, not a guaranteed offer. Specific rates originate from secondary comparison material and should be treated cautiously. [S4]

  • 01:00–04:00 off-peak: 10.361p/kWh
  • Normal hours: 20.22p/kWh
  • 16:00–19:00 peak: 30.22p/kWh
  • Standing charge: 55p/day

The key distinction

A home office consuming 10–20 kWh/day cannot simply be moved to midnight. But its total daytime demand does not automatically justify a battery.

The crucial question is how much import occurs during the tariff’s most expensive hours. A household with heavy 10:00–15:00 use but moderate 16:00–19:00 use may benefit from ToU pricing, while leaving a larger battery under-used.

Why a suitable ToU tariff is the best-value move

Central ToU calculation

  • 1,080 kWh overnight × 10.361p = about £112
  • 1,800 kWh peak × 30.22p = about £544
  • 6,120 kWh other hours × 20.22p = about £1,237
  • Standing charge = about £201
Total ≈ £2,094/year → saving ≈ £330/year

What makes a tariff suitable

The best tariff is not necessarily the one with the cheapest overnight window. It is the tariff with the lowest total annual cost for the home’s actual half-hourly pattern.

E.ON has described structures with lower-priced periods extending through much of the day, potentially suiting home-office demand better than an overnight-only arrangement. [S16] [S31]

Do not assume access to EV rates. EDF’s 6.49–6.99p/kWh overnight products are presented as EV tariffs, while Octopus Go and Intelligent Octopus Go generally require a qualifying EV or plug-in hybrid. E.ON Drive products are also EV-oriented unless the supplier confirms eligibility in writing. [S5] [S22] [S21] [S18]

Conventional Economy 7-style arrangements can be worse for a home-office household if daytime rates are materially higher and too little demand can move overnight. E.ON specifically highlights the importance of consumption timing for ToU outcomes. [S12] [S28] Promotional free-electricity events should not be treated as dependable savings: EDF says they are not guaranteed and depend on location and grid conditions. [S13]

Battery economics: why larger is not better here

~5 kWh battery: central model

  • 5.2 kWh nominal; 4.68 kWh usable
  • 2.6 kW continuous discharge limit
  • 90% round-trip efficiency
  • £4,050 installed cost
  • 88 kWh/year standby consumption
  • 80% usable capacity remaining in year 10

Indicative market material places systems of this size around £3,800–£4,300 installed. [S10] [S9]

~10 kWh battery: central model

  • 9.5 kWh nominal; 8.55 kWh usable
  • 3.6 kW continuous discharge limit
  • 90% round-trip efficiency
  • £6,150 installed cost
  • 88 kWh/year standby consumption
  • 80% usable capacity remaining in year 10

Indicative pricing is about £5,800–£6,500 installed, subject to inverter, electrical work, backup hardware and region. [S10]

5 kWh: £209 first-year net benefit

The model assumes 1,400 kWh delivered annually—about 299 equivalent full cycles—not a full daily cycle. Of that output, 75% displaces peak imports and 25% normal-rate imports.

Avoided price = (75% × 30.22p) + (25% × 20.22p) = 27.72p/kWh
Charge cost per delivered kWh = 10.361p ÷ 0.90 = 11.51p
1,400 × (27.72p − 11.51p) − ~£18 standby ≈ £209/year

At £4,050 installed, simple payback is roughly 19.4 years. Ten-year arbitrage benefit is approximately £1,850–£1,900 after degradation—well below capital cost.

10 kWh: similar saving, much higher cost

The model assumes 1,580 kWh delivered annually, or only about 185 equivalent full cycles. More capacity does not help if there is insufficient expensive-period demand to absorb it.

Avoided price = (55% × 30.22p) + (45% × 20.22p) = 25.72p/kWh
1,580 × (25.72p − 11.51p) − ~£18 standby ≈ £206/year

At £6,150 installed, simple payback is around 30 years. Over ten years, this system is likely to leave the household about £1,000 worse off than simply taking the ToU tariff.

Power is as important as capacity. A 2.6 kW battery cannot cover a simultaneous 4–6 kW domestic load; the remainder remains imported at the peak rate. Even 3.6 kW will not cover every appliance combination. [S10]

A reliably wide spread would improve the small-battery case. But it is not safe to assume for a ten-year asset: dynamic pricing can change, historic prices do not predict future costs, and eligibility or operating terms can be altered. [S21]

Installed cost matters most

To match ToU-only over ten years under central assumptions, the 5 kWh battery would need to cost close to its expected £1,850–£1,900 arbitrage value—perhaps around £2,000 if modest backup is valued. That is far below the indicative £3,800–£4,300 range. [S10]

Peak displacement matters

Moving all 1,400 kWh of small-battery output from a 75% peak-displacement mix to 100% peak import improves annual benefit by only around £35. Half-hourly load data is nevertheless crucial because the larger gain comes from avoiding low-value normal-rate discharge.

Degradation and repairs hurt

The model assumes 80% capacity in year 10. At 70%, the 5 kWh system’s ten-year arbitrage value falls by roughly £100–£150. No major repair or inverter replacement has been included; that omission favours the battery case. [S10]

Important practical constraints

Backup power is not automatic

A grid-connected battery may not run the home during an outage. Backup can require a gateway, critical-load consumer unit, isolation equipment and additional installation work. These additions can materially increase cost. [S9]

Warranty is not a return guarantee

Ask for exact capacity-retention, throughput, exclusions and labour-cover terms. A warranty may protect capacity only down to a threshold such as 70%, rather than guarantee a ten-year financial result. [S10]

Do not count export revenue

SEG is intended for qualifying renewable generation, not simply importing cheap grid electricity, storing it and exporting later. Written supplier confirmation is needed before assuming grid-charged battery export is permitted or paid. [S26] [S33]

VAT and grid rules need checking

No general battery-only grant is included. Confirm standalone-battery VAT treatment, DNO notification or approval, export limitation and system configuration with the installer. EDF’s solar material does not establish equivalent treatment for every standalone battery. [S27]

Why supplier and manufacturer savings claims are not used as the conclusion

GivEnergy promotes battery-only savings and a five-year return but does not publish the required assumptions on tariff rates, capacity, cycling, degradation, standing charges and household demand. [S25]

Supplier examples involving solar and batteries are also not transferable to a battery-only household because they include solar generation, self-consumption, import/export arrangements or export revenue. [S27] Octopus Flux, for example, requires solar alongside a battery and relevant import/export arrangements. [S8]

Before buying anything: a four-step evidence pack

1

Get interval data

Obtain 12 months of half-hourly smart-meter imports. Separate weekday, weekend, seasonal and 16:00–19:00 demand.

2

Price tariffs properly

Get at least three postcode-specific quotes including every unit rate, standing charge, duration, exit fee and eligibility term.

3

Switch tariff first

Test whether actual ToU savings appear without capital expenditure or a battery performance risk.

4

Quote batteries last

Only then request itemised, comparable quotes and model the battery against the tariff-only outcome.

  1. Analyse imports: 16:00–19:00 kWh, overnight demand, average and maximum demand, weekday/weekend patterns, seasons and office contribution.
  2. Confirm tariff rules: smart-meter requirements, battery eligibility, grid charging, export restrictions and whether automation is compulsory.
  3. Request itemised quotes: battery modules, inverter, labour, consumer-unit work, DNO work, monitoring, backup, VAT and export limitation.
  4. Read technical documents: usable capacity, continuous power, efficiency, standby draw, warranty throughput, retained capacity and post-warranty replacement cost.

Best-for verdicts

Best financial return: ToU without battery

Option 2. About £330/year in central-case savings, no equipment cost and no battery-performance risk.

Best simplicity: competitive flat tariff

Option 1. Sensible if ToU quotes are unattractive or data show too much unavoidable peak use.

Best conditional battery choice: ~5 kWh

Option 3. Only where a very low installed price, reliably wide price spread, strong evening demand and meaningful backup value all align.

Best avoided for arbitrage: ~10 kWh

Option 4. More expensive and more likely under-used; it becomes more plausible with later solar, EV, heat pump, electric heating or high evening load.

Final conclusion: for this home, a battery without solar panels is unlikely to be financially worthwhile on import-price arbitrage alone. The practical recommendation is to analyse half-hourly usage, obtain tariff quotations, switch tariff first, and buy a battery only if the evidence shows an unusually favourable case.
