# Is a Home Battery Worthwhile Without Solar Panels? A Financial Assessment for an English Household (July 2026)

---

## 1. Scope and Assumptions

**Household profile:** ~9,000 kWh/year electricity consumption; flat rate ~£0.247/kWh; gas heating; SMETS2 smart meter; home office using 10–20 kWh/day predominantly during daytime working hours; limited ability to shift consumption manually; property owner expecting to remain ≥10 years; primary goal is financial saving; no solar panels planned.

## 2. Installed Costs

Multiple installer sources converge on the following ranges (0% VAT, England, 2026):

| System | Low estimate | Mid estimate | High estimate |
|---|---|---|---|
| ~5 kWh installed | £2,500 [S9] | £3,500–£4,200 [S3][S6] | £5,000 [S8] |
| ~10 kWh installed | £4,500 [S9] | £5,000–£7,000 [S4][S7][S10] | £8,000–£9,500 [S8][S9] |

**Adopted central estimates:** £4,000 for a 5 kWh system; £6,500 for a 10 kWh system. These are conservative mid-points from installer sources [S3][S4][S6][S7], treated cautiously given commercial incentives. Installer quotes should be obtained before any decision.

**Important:** VAT rises from 0% to 5% after March 2027 [S6][S9], adding ~£200–£325 to costs if installation is delayed.

## 3. Tariff Options

### 3.1 Flat-rate baseline
Current flat rate: ~£0.247/kWh. Annual cost at 9,000 kWh: **£2,223/year** [S12 context].

### 3.2 Time-of-use tariffs (no battery required)
The most relevant tariff for this household is **Octopus Go**: off-peak rate ~8.5p/kWh (00:30–05:30, 5 hours), peak rate ~30.1p/kWh [S11]. A simpler alternative is **Economy 7** (~8–10p off-peak, ~20–24p daytime).

**Critical constraint for this household:** The home office consumes 10–20 kWh/day during daytime working hours. This consumption cannot be shifted to the off-peak window without a battery. On Octopus Go, daytime electricity costs ~30.1p/kWh — higher than the current flat rate of 24.7p/kWh. Switching to a TOU tariff *without* a battery could therefore **increase** costs for the majority of this household's consumption unless overnight usage (e.g., dishwasher, washing machine, EV if applicable) is substantial.

**Estimate for TOU without battery (9,000 kWh/year):**
- Assume 20% of consumption (1,800 kWh) can be shifted to off-peak overnight: saves (24.7p − 8.5p) × 1,800 = **£292/year**
- Remaining 80% (7,200 kWh) at 30.1p vs 24.7p: **costs an extra £389/year**
- **Net effect: approximately −£97/year** (i.e., slightly worse than flat rate)

This is a critical finding: for a high-daytime-usage household, switching to Octopus Go *without* a battery is likely to be marginally loss-making or at best neutral, not beneficial. Economy 7 produces a similar result. The tariff switch only becomes clearly beneficial when paired with a battery that can store cheap overnight electricity for daytime discharge.

## 4. Battery Scenarios: Financial Modelling

### 4.1 Core arbitrage mechanism
A battery charges during the off-peak window (8.5p/kWh on Octopus Go) and discharges during the day, displacing electricity that would otherwise cost 30.1p/kWh. The net saving per kWh displaced is:

**30.1p − (8.5p ÷ 0.90 round-trip efficiency) = 30.1p − 9.4p = ~20.7p/kWh**

### 4.2 Realistic daily cycling assumption
This household uses 10–20 kWh/day in the home office during daytime. A 5 kWh battery (4.05 kWh usable after losses) can displace roughly 4 kWh of daytime consumption per cycle. A 10 kWh battery (8.1 kWh usable) can displace roughly 8 kWh. Given the high daytime demand, the battery can realistically discharge fully each day — **full daily cycling is supportable for this household** [S4][S5], unlike lower-consumption households where this assumption would be questionable.

However, the 5-hour off-peak window (00:30–05:30) must also supply overnight direct consumption (e.g., fridge, standby loads ~0.5–1 kWh). This reduces available charging capacity slightly but does not materially affect the model.

**Assumed cycles per year:** 330 (allowing ~35 days for partial cycles, grid outages, or tariff changes).

### 4.3 Annual savings calculation (Year 1, before degradation)

| Scenario | Effective kWh/cycle | Saving/kWh | Cycles/yr | Gross saving | Less: off-peak charging cost premium vs flat | **Net annual saving** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 kWh battery + Go | 4.05 kWh | 20.7p | 330 | £276 | Already included in arbitrage spread | **~£276** |
| 10 kWh battery + Go | 8.10 kWh | 20.7p | 330 | £553 | Already included | **~£553** |

*Note: The arbitrage spread already accounts for the cost of charging at 8.5p vs displacing 30.1p electricity, so no separate adjustment is needed. The flat-rate comparison is embedded in the 20.7p net saving.*

**Adjustment for daytime non-office loads:** Some daytime consumption (cooking, lighting) cannot be timed to coincide with battery discharge. Assume the battery can realistically serve ~70% of its discharged energy as useful displacement. Adjusted savings:

- 5 kWh system: ~£193/year
- 10 kWh system: ~£387/year

### 4.4 Degradation adjustment
Capacity degrades ~2%/year. Over 10 years, average usable capacity is approximately 90% of Year 1. Applying this to savings:

- 5 kWh: 10-year cumulative saving ≈ £193 × 9.5 years effective = **~£1,834**
- 10 kWh: 10-year cumulative saving ≈ £387 × 9.5 = **~£3,677**

### 4.5 Costs over 10 years

| Item | 5 kWh | 10 kWh |
|---|---|---|
| Installation (central estimate) | £4,000 | £6,500 |
| Maintenance (10 yr) | £500 | £500 |
| Replacement within 10 yr? | Unlikely (10–12 yr life) [S6] | Unlikely | 
| **Total cost** | **£4,500** | **£7,000** |

### 4.6 10-year net financial outcome

| Option | 10-yr saving | 10-yr cost | **Net outcome** | Simple payback |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flat rate, no battery | £0 | £0 | Baseline | — |
| TOU tariff, no battery | −£970 (loss) | £0 | **−£970** | N/A |
| 5 kWh battery + Go | £1,834 | £4,500 | **−£2,666** | ~23 years |
| 10 kWh battery + Go | £3,677 | £7,000 | **−£3,323** | ~18 years |

## 5. Sensitivity Analysis

### 5.1 Annual consumption

| Consumption | TOU no battery | 5 kWh net (10yr) | 10 kWh net (10yr) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6,000 kWh/yr | ~−£650 | ~−£2,800 | ~−£3,500 |
| 9,000 kWh/yr | ~−£970 | ~−£2,666 | ~−£3,323 |
| 11,000 kWh/yr | ~−£1,185 | ~−£2,550 | ~−£3,100 |

Higher consumption worsens the TOU-without-battery outcome (more daytime units at expensive peak rate) but only marginally improves battery outcomes since the battery capacity is fixed.

### 5.2 Tariff spread sensitivity

| Off-peak rate | Peak rate | Net saving/kWh | 10 kWh annual saving | 10-yr net |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7p (Intelligent Go) | 30.1p | 22.6p | ~£603 | ~−£2,280 |
| 8.5p (Go) | 30.1p | 20.7p | ~£553 | ~−£3,323 |
| 10p (Economy 7) | 24.7p (flat equiv) | 13.6p | ~£363 | ~−£3,563 |
| 8.5p | 35p (future increase) | 26.1p | ~£697 | ~−£2,370 |

Even under the most favourable spread (7p/30p+), the 10 kWh battery does not pay back within 10 years at central cost estimates.

### 5.3 Installation cost sensitivity (10 kWh battery)

| Install cost | 10-yr saving | **Net 10-yr outcome** | Payback |
|---|---|---|---|
| £4,500 (low) | £3,677 | **−£823** | ~12 years |
| £6,500 (central) | £3,677 | **−£3,323** | ~18 years |
| £8,000 (high) | £3,677 | **−£4,823** | ~22 years |

At the low end of installation costs (£4,500), the 10 kWh battery approaches breakeven over 12 years — just outside the 10-year window.

### 5.4 Peak consumption proportion sensitivity
If the household can shift more consumption to off-peak (e.g., 40% rather than 20%):
- TOU without battery becomes marginally positive (~+£50–£100/year)
- Battery savings increase modestly (~10–15%)
- Neither changes the fundamental conclusion within 10 years

### 5.5 Degradation sensitivity
If degradation is 1%/year (optimistic): 10-year cumulative saving increases ~5%. If 3%/year: decreases ~5%. Not material to the conclusion.

## 6. Additional Risk Factors

**Tariff uncertainty:** Octopus Go rates are not guaranteed. If the off-peak rate rises or the peak rate falls (e.g., due to further price cap reductions [S12]), the arbitrage spread narrows. Electricity policy levies have been reduced and promised stable until 2029 [S12], which slightly suppresses the peak rate and thus the spread.

**Manufacturer stability:** GivEnergy entered administration in 2026 [S10], illustrating that battery manufacturer continuity is a real risk affecting warranty claims and software support.

**Inverter replacement:** Hybrid inverters may require replacement within 10–15 years, adding £500–£1,500 to costs not fully captured above.

**Agile tariff:** Octopus Agile offers half-hourly pricing with potentially wider spreads, but requires automated smart charging to exploit and introduces price volatility risk. Not recommended without battery management software.

## 7. Conclusions

### Does either battery pay for itself within 10 years?
**No.** At central cost estimates, neither a 5 kWh (~23-year payback) nor a 10 kWh (~18-year payback) battery pays back within 10 years through tariff arbitrage alone, without solar panels. Only at the low end of installation costs (£4,500 for 10 kWh) does payback approach 12 years — still outside the window. [S3][S4][S5][S6][S8][S10]

### Does switching tariff without a battery provide meaningful savings?
**No — and it may cost more.** For this household, with high daytime consumption that cannot be shifted, switching to Octopus Go without a battery is likely to be marginally loss-making (~−£97–£200/year) because the majority of consumption falls in the expensive peak window. This is the most important finding for this household specifically. [S11]

### Conditions under which the conclusion changes
- Installation cost falls to ~£4,000–£4,500 for a 10 kWh system (possible with competitive quotes or future price falls)
- The tariff spread widens significantly (e.g., off-peak 7p, peak 35p+)
- The household installs solar panels (fundamentally changes the economics — outside scope)
- Electricity prices rise substantially above current cap levels, increasing the value of stored cheap electricity
- The household's daytime consumption profile changes, allowing more shifting

### Best financial option
**Remain on a competitive flat-rate tariff and take no further action.** This avoids both the capital outlay of a battery and the risk of higher bills from a TOU tariff without the ability to shift daytime consumption. The opportunity cost of the battery capital (£4,000–£6,500) invested elsewhere is also relevant.

**Second-best:** If the household can genuinely shift 30–40% of consumption to overnight (e.g., through timers, overnight EV charging, or changed working patterns), a TOU tariff without a battery becomes marginally positive and worth pursuing at zero capital cost.

### Strongest argument against this recommendation
The strongest counter-argument is **optionality and future-proofing**: battery costs are falling, tariff spreads may widen, and a battery installed now at 0% VAT (before March 2027 [S6][S9]) positions the household to benefit from future solar installation or vehicle-to-grid tariffs. The backup power benefit, while secondary, also has real value. If an installer quote comes in at £4,000–£4,500 for a 10 kWh system, the financial case becomes materially less negative.

### What to obtain before any purchase
1. **Smart meter consumption data:** Download half-hourly usage data (available via Octopus, n3rgy, or DCC) to establish exactly how much consumption falls in the 00:30–05:30 window versus daytime peak hours.
2. **At least three installer quotes** for both 5 kWh and 10 kWh systems, specifying LFP chemistry, AC-coupled configuration, and full installed cost including inverter.
3. **Tariff modelling:** Use Octopus Energy's own switching tool or an independent tariff comparison (e.g., Look After My Bills) to model actual bill impact of Octopus Go given your specific half-hourly profile before committing.
4. **Warranty documentation:** Confirm manufacturer warranty terms, what happens if the manufacturer ceases trading [S10], and whether the installer offers an extended warranty.
5. **Battery management system capability:** Confirm the system can be programmed to charge only during the off-peak window and discharge to meet daytime load automatically, without manual intervention.
