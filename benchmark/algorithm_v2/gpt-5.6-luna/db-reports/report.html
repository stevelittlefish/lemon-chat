Is a home battery worthwhile without solar panels?

For this English household, grid-charged storage is technically possible—but the numbers favour switching tariff first, not buying a battery.

Executive answer

Battery: possible, but not normally worthwhile at current prices

A home battery can operate without solar: it charges from the grid during cheap periods and discharges when electricity is dearer. Suppliers and manufacturers confirm that this operating model is possible, subject to tariff terms, battery software and installation design [S34] [S44] [S24].

But advertisements for solar-and-battery systems often overstate what storage can save here. Much of this home’s demand occurs during daytime home-office hours. That electricity may already be bought at an ordinary shoulder rate and cannot necessarily be shifted through a battery.

Recommendation: download at least 12 months of half-hourly data, test a suitable time-of-use tariff first, and buy a battery only if measured peak demand, power capability, tariff spread and installed cost together show a realistic ten-year return.

The options

What each route is actually good for

01 · Competitive flat tariff

Simplicity and certainty

At 24.7p/kWh, the modelled usage charge is £2,223/year. There is no capital investment, degradation, automation or tariff-control risk.

Best when

  • Overnight consumption is very small.
  • Peak use is unavoidable.
  • A ToU tariff has a punitive daytime or evening rate.
  • Battery-only eligibility cannot be confirmed.

A sensible fallback, but not the strongest base-case financial result.

02 · ToU tariff without battery

Best financial option

Directly moving flexible loads into cheap periods captures savings without capital cost or round-trip losses. The modelled bill falls to £2,025/year: about £198/year saved, or £1,980 over ten years before tariff changes.

Watch-outs

  • Daytime office demand is hard to move.
  • Peak rates, hours and standing charges may erase the headline saving.
  • Some attractive offers are EV-linked or require a compatible charger [S14] [S21] [S22].

Test this route for several months using real half-hourly data.

03 · Approximately 5 kWh battery + ToU

Most defensible battery size

With 4.5 kWh usable capacity, 300 useful cycles and 90% efficiency, the model estimates about 1,200 kWh of delivered battery energy annually and about £240/year of extra saving.

At £3,700 installed, £3,700 ÷ £240 ≈ 15.4 years. That is longer than many warranties and leaves an estimated £1,500 loss over ten years versus ToU alone.

Buy only if

  • 3–4.5 kWh of regular peak demand is evidenced.
  • Continuous discharge power covers the required load.
  • A no-solar tariff permits grid charging and automated control.
  • The quote is close to £2,200 or less for base-case economics.

04 · Approximately 10 kWh battery + ToU

Backup-led choice

With approximately 9 kWh usable capacity, the model estimates about 1,470 kWh delivered annually and £294/year of battery-specific saving.

The extra 5 kWh adds only £54/year over the 5 kWh option, while adding roughly £2,150 to midpoint capital cost. At £5,850, payback is approximately 19.9 years.

Appropriate only if

  • 7–9 kWh is regularly consumed during high-price periods.
  • Longer backup duration is a major objective.
  • The spread is unusually large and cycling is almost daily.
  • The quotation is substantially below the normal range.

Shared considerations

Five details that can make or break the calculation

Tariff eligibility

Do not assume a solar tariff is available to a grid-charged battery. Flux products are designed around solar and batteries [S16] [S17]. EV tariffs may require a car or charger [S13] [S14].

Obtain written confirmation that standalone grid charging, automated control, discharge and any export are permitted.

Usable capacity and power

Nominal kWh is not delivered kWh. Check usable capacity, reserve, continuous and peak power, low-state-of-charge behaviour and realistic round-trip efficiency.

A 10 kWh battery at 2 kW takes five hours to empty; at 5 kW it can serve a much larger instantaneous load.

Cycles and degradation

A complete cycle every day is not guaranteed. The base case uses 300 useful days and a 2% annual saving decline.

“Ten-year warranty” may include throughput, cycle and end-of-warranty capacity limits rather than unrestricted original performance [S55].

Backup is a separate benefit

Backup may require a gateway, protected circuits and reserve capacity. Whole-home backup should not be assumed to be included in the advertised battery price [S47].

It can justify a battery for resilience, but it should not be counted as bill saving.

VAT and grants

No battery grant is assumed. The Boiler Upgrade Scheme concerns heat pumps and biomass boilers, not standalone batteries [S39].

Professionally supplied and installed qualifying materials may receive 0% VAT under relevant rules on 14 July 2026; separately bought batteries may be standard-rated. Check the quotation and HMRC guidance [S26].

Export income

SEG is primarily for eligible renewable generation. The Feed-in Tariff closed to new applications in 2019 [S52].

Do not include solar export income in a battery-only model unless the supplier explicitly confirms legal and economic eligibility [S37] [S15].

Decision checklist

What to obtain before purchasing

1 · Analyse the meter

Download at least 12 months of half-hourly smart-meter data and identify:

  • Overnight consumption and loads that can shift directly.
  • Consumption in each proposed peak window.
  • Home-office power and maximum simultaneous load.
  • Days when a valuable cycle is realistically possible.
  • Energy already consumed during cheap periods.

2 · Get tariff terms in writing

Request postcode-specific quotations showing:

  • Every unit rate, time window and standing charge.
  • Eligibility, exit fees and price-change terms.
  • Standalone-battery and grid-charging permission.
  • Automated charge/discharge support and software requirements.
  • Export rules and restrictions on grid-charged electricity.

3 · Compare at least three itemised battery quotations

  • Model and usable capacity
  • Inverter model
  • Continuous and peak charge/discharge power
  • Round-trip efficiency
  • Reserve setting
  • Warranty, throughput and end capacity
  • Expected degradation
  • Backup gateway and protected circuits
  • Installation, cabling and DNO work
  • Monitoring or software fees
  • Maintenance and replacement assumptions
  • VAT treatment
Decision rule:
purchase only when the measured peak load can be served at the required power, the tariff is confirmed compatible, and the installed price produces an acceptable ten-year outcome after losses, degradation and likely replacement risk.
Final verdict

Switch tariff first

For this English household, a home battery without solar is technically viable but not normally financially worthwhile at current indicative prices.

The 5 kWh system is the more plausible battery choice, but its expected payback is around 15 years and its ten-year result is negative compared with simply changing tariff. The 10 kWh system is weaker because daytime-heavy demand creates too little additional high-value discharge opportunity.

A suitable ToU tariff without a battery is likely to save approximately £198/year in the illustrative model while avoiding capital cost, losses, degradation and software risk. The conclusion could change with a durable 25–30p/kWh effective spread, almost-daily cycling, much greater evening demand, flexibility-market income backed by contract, or a substantially lower quotation. Future flexibility policy recognises storage’s potential, but uncertain future payments should not be included without a firm revenue stream [S27] [S28].

Bottom line

Measure first. Switch tariff second. Buy storage only when the household’s own half-hourly profile and a fully itemised quotation prove that it is worth doing.
