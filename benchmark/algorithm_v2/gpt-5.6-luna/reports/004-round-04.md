# Updated assessment: home battery without solar in England

## Conclusion

For this household, **switching to a suitable time-of-use (ToU) tariff without buying a battery is the best financial option**. On the illustrative assumptions, it saves about **£200 a year** with no capital risk.

A battery can exploit the difference between cheap overnight and expensive peak electricity, but this home’s unusually high **daytime** office demand limits the benefit. A battery charged overnight cannot normally displace electricity used during working hours unless the tariff’s discharge period includes those hours and the battery has sufficient stored energy and power.

On the model below, neither a 5 kWh nor a 10 kWh battery recovers its own installed cost within ten years. A battery could nevertheless be justified for backup resilience, provided that benefit is valued separately.

## Tariff and regulatory context

The household’s current rate of 24.7p/kWh is already competitive against the illustrative July 2026 price-cap benchmark. Octopus advertises Intelligent Octopus Go with six hours at 8p/kWh and reports a July 2026 average capped unit rate of 26.11p/kWh, excluding standing charges and varying by region [S13]. However, Go is principally an EV tariff: Octopus says that an EV and home charger, or a compatible vehicle/charger, is required [S21][S22]. Historical information suggested that all household electricity could receive the overnight rate, but this should not be assumed to remain available to a standalone battery [S24].

Other suppliers advertise overnight or battery-oriented tariffs. British Gas confirms that smart-meter tariffs can offer cheaper overnight electricity and that its battery tariff is intended to charge a battery overnight and use it later [S31]. EDF advertises indicative overnight rates of approximately 6.49–6.99p/kWh [S36]. These pages do not establish the precise July 2026 rates, eligibility conditions or standing charges for this property.

Before relying on a tariff, obtain written confirmation that:

- a standalone battery is eligible;
- grid charging is permitted;
- the supplier will not restrict or penalise battery operation;
- the relevant overnight, daytime and peak rates and hours are fixed or variable;
- standing charges, control requirements and cancellation terms are understood.

Agile-style tariffs may create larger opportunities but have volatile half-hourly prices and generally require automated control [S18]. Solar-linked tariffs such as Flux are not suitable evidence because they require solar panels; availability may also change [S25].

No general battery grant is assumed. The Warm Homes Plan discusses batteries and flexibility but does not establish a currently available subsidy applicable here [S28]. A professionally supplied and installed qualifying battery may receive 0% VAT under HMRC’s energy-saving-material relief on 14 July 2026, subject to the precise contract and conditions; the position is scheduled to change to 5% from 1 April 2027, while a separately purchased battery is generally standard-rated [S26]. The quotation must state the VAT treatment.

## Base-case assumptions

The following is an indicative model, not a tariff quotation:

- Annual electricity use: **9,000 kWh**.
- Existing flat tariff: **24.7p/kWh**.
- ToU rates: **9p overnight, 24.5p daytime/shoulder and 30p peak**.
- Demand profile: **60% daytime, 20% peak/evening and 20% overnight**.
- Standing charges: excluded and assumed identical. Actual bills must include them.
- Round-trip battery efficiency: **90%**.
- Usable capacity: **4.5 kWh** from a nominal 5 kWh battery; **9 kWh** from a nominal 10 kWh battery.
- Battery power: assumed sufficient to charge overnight and discharge the relevant peak load. Actual continuous and peak charge/discharge ratings could materially change the result.
- Battery charging is prioritised only when the expected peak saving exceeds charging cost and losses.
- Annual battery discharge: approximately **1,200 kWh for 5 kWh** and **1,470 kWh for 10 kWh**. This represents roughly 300 useful operating days, not a full cycle every day. It allows for direct overnight consumption, limited peak demand, power constraints and unprofitable days.
- Battery savings decline by **2% annually** for degradation.
- Installed costs: **£3,200–£4,200** for 5 kWh and **£4,600–£7,100** for 10 kWh, with midpoint costs of £3,700 and £5,850 [S12].
- No maintenance, finance charges or replacement within ten years: an optimistic assumption.

The model’s effective battery value is approximately 20p per delivered kWh: 30p avoided peak electricity less about 10p/kWh effective overnight charging cost after losses.

### Ten-year outcomes

Using a 2% annual decline in battery savings, the ten-year multiplier is approximately 9.15.

| Option | Ten-year saving versus current flat tariff | Ten-year capital cost | Net ten-year outcome |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Flat tariff | £0 | £0 | £0 |
| ToU only | £1,980 | £0 | **+£1,980** |
| 5 kWh + ToU, midpoint cost | £4,176 | £3,700 | **+£476** |
| 10 kWh + ToU, midpoint cost | £4,670 | £5,850 | **–£1,180** |

The battery-only element is negative at midpoint prices:

- 5 kWh: £240 × 9.15 − £3,700 = **approximately –£1,500**;
- 10 kWh: £294 × 9.15 − £5,850 = **approximately –£3,160**.

Thus the 5 kWh option may produce a positive overall ten-year result only because it includes the benefit of switching tariff. It does **not** repay its battery cost compared with taking the ToU tariff alone. Simple battery payback is approximately **13–18 years for 5 kWh** and **16–24 years for 10 kWh**.

The calculation also excludes inverter or battery replacement, maintenance, financing, tariff switching costs and adverse tariff changes. Including them would worsen the result.

## Main sensitivities

| Effective peak/off-peak spread | 5 kWh annual saving | 10 kWh annual saving |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 10p/kWh | £107 | £131 |
| 20p/kWh | £240 | £294 |

At a sustained 30p/kWh spread, midpoint simple payback is still approximately 11 years for 5 kWh and 14 years for 10 kWh. A narrower spread makes the investment substantially worse; unusually volatile prices could improve it if automated controls reliably capture high-price periods.

If only 10% of consumption occurs during peak hours, annual savings fall to roughly £120 and £180 for 5 kWh and 10 kWh. At 30% peak consumption they rise to about £240 and £360, although the 5 kWh unit becomes more capacity-limited.

At base utilisation, the maximum capital cost consistent with a ten-year battery-only payback is approximately **£2,200 for 5 kWh** and **£2,700 for 10 kWh**, before replacement costs. Faster degradation of 3–4%, limited warranty throughput or inverter replacement reduces those limits.

## Recommendation

1. **First obtain 12 months of half-hourly smart-meter data** and test the actual property against competitive flat and ToU tariffs, including standing charges.
2. **Switch tariff without buying a battery** if the measured saving is worthwhile and the supplier confirms eligibility. This is the lowest-risk and most likely financial improvement.
3. Consider a **5 kWh battery** only with a low quotation, strong warranty, automated control, a persistently large spread and substantial evening demand.
4. Do not select a **10 kWh battery merely because total annual consumption is high**. It requires evidence that the extra usable capacity can regularly discharge during expensive periods.

The strongest argument against this recommendation is that future wholesale-price volatility and flexibility tariffs could create much larger arbitrage opportunities than the conservative model assumes. The counter-risk is that tariffs, supplier rules and software may change, spreads may narrow, degradation may exceed expectations and replacement costs may arise. Battery-only installation is technically feasible [S34], but marketing claims and typical-household examples should not be substituted for this home’s interval data [S29][S32][S33].

Before purchase, obtain at least three itemised quotations specifying usable—not nominal—capacity, continuous and peak power, round-trip efficiency, warranty and throughput limits, capacity-retention guarantee, degradation assumptions, reserve settings, backup hardware and outage performance, monitoring or software fees, VAT, DNO requirements, maintenance and replacement pricing. Obtain the supplier’s written tariff rules and confirm how much of the office load actually falls within the tariff’s peak period.
