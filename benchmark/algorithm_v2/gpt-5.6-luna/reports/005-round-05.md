# Updated assessment: home battery without solar in England

## Conclusion

For this household, **switching to a suitable time-of-use (ToU) tariff without buying a battery is the strongest financial option**. On the illustrative assumptions, it reduces annual electricity cost by about **£200**, with no installation cost or battery-performance risk.

A battery can save money by charging cheaply overnight and discharging during expensive periods. However, this home’s unusually high **daytime** office consumption weakens the case: electricity stored overnight cannot help with daytime use unless the tariff permits profitable discharge then, and the battery has enough stored energy and power to meet that demand.

On the base-case model, neither a 5 kWh nor a 10 kWh battery pays for itself compared with taking the ToU tariff alone. A battery may still be worthwhile for backup power, but that should be treated as a separate non-financial benefit.

## Tariffs, eligibility and policy

The existing flat rate of **24.7p/kWh** is already competitive. Octopus reports a July 2026 average capped unit rate of 26.11p/kWh and advertises six-hour overnight electricity at 8p/kWh, although Intelligent Octopus Go is principally an EV tariff and normally requires a compatible EV and charger [S13][S21][S22]. Historic availability of cheap overnight electricity for all household consumption should not be assumed to continue for a standalone battery [S24].

British Gas confirms that smart-meter tariffs can provide cheaper overnight electricity and markets tariffs intended to charge batteries overnight and discharge them later [S31]. EDF advertises indicative overnight rates around 6.49–6.99p/kWh, but these pages do not establish the precise rates, eligibility rules, peak periods or standing charges applicable to this property [S36].

Before relying on a tariff, obtain written confirmation that:

- a standalone, grid-charged battery is eligible;
- grid charging and export are permitted;
- the supplier will not restrict battery operation;
- the overnight, daytime and peak rates and hours are known;
- standing charges, control requirements, cancellation terms and future price changes are understood.

Agile tariffs may offer larger opportunities but expose the household to volatile half-hourly prices and generally require reliable automated control [S18]. Solar-linked tariffs are not relevant evidence because this household does not have solar panels [S25].

The Smart Export Guarantee concerns eligible renewable generation. It is not an ordinary subsidy for importing cheap electricity, and EDF’s battery-only export conditions require separate certification and compliance with its tariff terms [S37]. The Boiler Upgrade Scheme supports heat pumps and biomass boilers, not batteries [S39]. No general battery grant is therefore assumed.

A professionally supplied and installed qualifying battery may have benefited from 0% VAT on 14 July 2026 under HMRC’s energy-saving-material relief, subject to the precise contract and conditions; the position is scheduled to change to 5% from 1 April 2027. A separately purchased battery may be standard-rated [S26]. The quotation should state the VAT treatment explicitly.

## Base-case assumptions

The indicative model assumes:

- Annual consumption: **9,000 kWh**
- Existing flat rate: **24.7p/kWh**
- ToU rates: **9p/kWh overnight, 24.5p daytime/shoulder and 30p peak**
- Demand profile: **60% daytime, 20% peak/evening and 20% overnight**
- Standing charges: excluded and assumed identical
- 5 kWh battery: **4.5 kWh usable capacity**
- 10 kWh battery: **9 kWh usable capacity**
- Round-trip efficiency: **90%**
- Battery power: sufficient for the assumed charging and peak discharge, subject to verification
- Annual discharged energy: approximately **1,200 kWh for 5 kWh** and **1,470 kWh for 10 kWh**
- Battery savings decline by **2% each year**
- Installed cost: **£3,200–£4,200** for 5 kWh and **£4,600–£7,100** for 10 kWh, with midpoint costs of £3,700 and £5,850 [S12]
- No maintenance, financing or replacement during ten years

The operating assumption is approximately 300 useful operating days per year, not a full cycle every day. It allows for nights when some electricity is consumed directly, insufficient profitable peak demand, power limits, control failures and days when the price spread is too small.

At a 30p peak price and 9p overnight price, the gross arbitrage value is reduced by charging losses. The model uses an approximate battery value of **20p per delivered kWh**, rather than treating all nominal capacity as useful.

## Ten-year outcome

Using a 2% annual decline in battery savings, the ten-year present-value-free multiplier is approximately 9.15.

| Option | Ten-year saving versus current flat tariff | Capital cost | Net ten-year outcome |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Flat tariff | £0 | £0 | £0 |
| ToU only | £1,980 | £0 | **+£1,980** |
| 5 kWh battery plus ToU | £4,176 | £3,700 midpoint | **+£476** |
| 10 kWh battery plus ToU | £4,670 | £5,850 midpoint | **–£1,180** |

This comparison can be misleading because the battery options include the benefit of changing tariff. Compared with taking the ToU tariff alone, the battery-only element is:

- 5 kWh: £240 × 9.15 − £3,700 = approximately **–£1,500**
- 10 kWh: £294 × 9.15 − £5,850 = approximately **–£3,160**

Simple battery payback is approximately **13–18 years** for 5 kWh and **16–24 years** for 10 kWh. Inverter replacement, maintenance, software fees, financing, additional electrical work or early battery replacement would worsen these results. Warranty throughput limits and capacity guarantees also matter.

## Key sensitivities

Approximate annual battery savings under different effective peak/off-peak spreads are:

| Effective spread | 5 kWh | 10 kWh |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 10p/kWh | £107 | £131 |
| 20p/kWh | £240 | £294 |
| 30p/kWh | £347 | £425 |

At a sustained 30p/kWh spread, midpoint payback remains approximately 11 years for 5 kWh and 14 years for 10 kWh. A narrower spread makes the investment substantially worse. Agile tariffs could improve returns if automation consistently captures high-price periods, but future wholesale prices, supplier rules and tariff availability are uncertain. Government analysis also identifies future market, technology, policy and consumer-behaviour uncertainty [S40][S41][S42][S43].

If only 10% of consumption occurs during peak hours, annual savings fall to roughly £120 and £180 for the two battery sizes. At 30% peak consumption they rise to approximately £240 and £360, although the 5 kWh unit becomes more capacity-limited.

At base utilisation, a battery-only ten-year payback would require an installed price of roughly **£2,200 or less for 5 kWh** and **£2,700 or less for 10 kWh**, before replacement costs. Faster degradation of 3–4%, lower usable capacity, power restrictions or an inverter replacement reduce those allowable prices.

## Recommendation

1. Obtain 12 months of half-hourly smart-meter data and compare actual bills under competitive flat and ToU tariffs, including standing charges.
2. If eligible, switch to the best suitable ToU tariff without a battery. This is the lowest-risk and most likely financial saving.
3. Consider a 5 kWh battery only if the quotation is unusually low, the tariff has a durable spread, automated control is included, and the home has substantial evening or other high-price demand.
4. Do not choose a 10 kWh battery merely because annual consumption is high. It requires evidence that the additional capacity can regularly discharge during expensive periods.

The strongest argument against this recommendation is that future price volatility and flexibility markets could make automated battery arbitrage substantially more valuable than this conservative model. The counterargument is that tariff terms, supplier eligibility, software, degradation and replacement costs could move in the opposite direction.

Before deciding, obtain interval consumption data and at least three itemised quotations specifying usable capacity, continuous and peak charge/discharge power, round-trip efficiency, warranty and throughput limits, capacity-retention guarantee, reserve settings, backup hardware and outage performance, monitoring or software fees, VAT, DNO requirements, maintenance and replacement pricing. Confirm precisely how much office demand falls within the tariff’s peak period.
