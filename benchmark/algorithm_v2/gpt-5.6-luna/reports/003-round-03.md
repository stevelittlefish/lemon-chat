# Updated assessment: home battery without solar in England

## Bottom line

For this household, **switching to a suitable time-of-use (ToU) tariff without buying a battery is the strongest financial option**. It could save roughly **£200 a year** under the illustrative assumptions below, although the actual result depends on tariff eligibility, standing charges and the household’s half-hourly demand.

A battery can buy cheap overnight electricity and discharge it during expensive periods, but this home’s unusually high daytime office consumption limits the opportunity. Electricity used during working hours generally cannot be displaced by a battery that charges overnight and discharges in the evening. On the assumptions modelled, **neither a 5 kWh nor a 10 kWh battery repays its installed cost within ten years**. Backup power could still justify one, but is a separate resilience benefit rather than a financial one.

## Tariffs and regulatory position

Octopus advertises Intelligent Octopus Go with six hours at 8p/kWh and states a July 2026 average price-cap unit rate of 26.11p/kWh, excluding standing charges and varying by region [S13]. However, the tariff is principally an EV product: Octopus describes Go as requiring an EV and home charger, while Intelligent Go requires a compatible car or charger and may control charging [S21][S22]. Historical Octopus information indicates that all household electricity, not only EV charging, could use the overnight rate, but it does not establish that the tariff remains available on those terms in July 2026 [S24].

Accordingly, an apparently attractive EV tariff must not be treated as available to a standalone battery without **written confirmation from the supplier**. Confirm eligibility, whether grid-charging and automated battery operation are allowed, the regional peak rate, standing charge and any cancellation or control conditions. Flux and Intelligent Flux are not suitable evidence for this case because they require solar panels; Intelligent Flux was also temporarily unavailable because of market volatility [S25]. Agile-type tariffs can produce larger opportunities but have volatile half-hourly prices and generally require automated control [S18].

There is no export income in this assessment. No general battery grant is assumed. The Warm Homes Plan discusses batteries and flexibility but does not establish a currently available subsidy or the financial terms applicable to this household [S28]. A professionally supplied and installed qualifying battery should generally receive 0% VAT under HMRC’s energy-saving-material relief on 14 July 2026, subject to the precise contract and conditions; installations are scheduled to return to 5% VAT from 1 April 2027, while a separately purchased battery is standard-rated [S26]. The quotation should state the VAT treatment.

## Calculation assumptions

These are illustrative, not a current tariff quotation:

- Annual consumption: 9,000 kWh; sensitivities at 6,000 and 11,000 kWh.
- Existing flat tariff: 24.7p/kWh.
- Illustrative ToU prices: 9p overnight, 24.5p daytime/shoulder and 30p peak.
- Demand profile: 60% daytime, 20% peak/evening and 20% overnight. This reflects substantial daytime office use.
- Standing charges: excluded, assumed equal. They must be included in a real comparison.
- Battery round-trip efficiency: 90%; delivering 1 kWh requires approximately 1.11 kWh of charging electricity.
- Usable capacity: 4.5 kWh from a nominal 5 kWh unit and 9 kWh from a nominal 10 kWh unit.
- The battery discharges only against otherwise expensive peak demand. It cannot economically serve daytime demand if it has already been used or if its power rating is insufficient.
- Annual delivered energy: approximately 1,200 kWh for the 5 kWh battery and 1,470 kWh for the 10 kWh battery. This represents around 300 useful operating days, not a full cycle every day. It allows for direct overnight consumption, insufficient peak demand, charging/discharging limits and unprofitable price periods.
- Battery savings decline by 2% annually for degradation; the ten-year savings multiplier is approximately 9.15.
- Installed costs: £3,200–£4,200 for 5 kWh and £4,600–£7,100 for 10 kWh, using midpoint costs of £3,700 and £5,850 [S12].
- No maintenance, finance cost or replacement within ten years is included. This is optimistic; inverter or battery replacement would worsen the result.

## Key sensitivities

The base case provides approximately 20p/kWh of gross battery value: 30p peak electricity avoided less roughly 10p/kWh effective charging cost after losses.

| Effective peak/off-peak spread | 5 kWh annual saving | 10 kWh annual saving |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 10p/kWh | £107 | £131 |
| 20p/kWh | £240 | £294 |
| 30p/kWh | £347 | £425 |

At a sustained 30p spread, midpoint simple payback remains roughly 11 years for 5 kWh and 14 years for 10 kWh. A sustained narrowing of the spread makes the battery substantially worse; volatile Agile prices could improve results only if automated control captures enough high-price periods.

If only 10% of consumption occurs during peak hours, savings fall to approximately £120 and £180 annually for 5 kWh and 10 kWh. At 30% peak consumption they rise to approximately £240 and £360, but the 5 kWh battery becomes more capacity-limited. More frequent cycling, higher peak demand and lower installed prices improve the case.

At the base utilisation and degradation assumptions, the maximum capital cost consistent with a ten-year payback is only about **£2,200 for 5 kWh** and **£2,700 for 10 kWh**, before allowing for replacement. Battery degradation of 3–4% rather than 2%, a shorter warranty, or an inverter replacement would reduce these limits further.

## Recommendation and decision checklist

1. Obtain at least 12 months of half-hourly smart-meter data and model actual bills under competitive flat and eligible ToU tariffs, including standing charges.
2. Switch to ToU without a battery if the supplier confirms eligibility and the measured saving remains positive. This is likely to provide meaningful but modest savings from naturally occurring overnight demand, not necessarily from the daytime office load.
3. Consider a 5 kWh battery only with a quotation near or below the lower end of the range, frequent evening demand, automated tariff control and strong warranty terms.
4. Do not choose 10 kWh merely because annual consumption is high. It is justified only if interval data shows that its additional usable capacity can regularly discharge during expensive periods.

The strongest argument against this recommendation is that future wholesale volatility could create much larger spreads for an automated battery. Conversely, tariffs may become less favourable, supplier eligibility may change, software may fail, degradation may exceed expectations and replacement costs may arise.

Before purchase, obtain the homeowner’s half-hourly consumption data; postcode-specific tariff rates and standing charges; written confirmation of standalone-battery eligibility and grid charging; at least three itemised quotations; usable rather than nominal capacity; continuous and peak charge/discharge power; efficiency; warranty and throughput limits; capacity-retention guarantee; reserve settings; backup hardware and outage operation; software or maintenance fees; VAT treatment; DNO requirements; and battery/inverter replacement pricing.
