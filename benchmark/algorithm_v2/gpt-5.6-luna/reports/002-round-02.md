# Updated assessment: home battery without solar in England

## Conclusion

For this household, **switching to a suitable time-of-use (ToU) tariff without buying a battery is the best first financial move**. A battery can exploit cheap overnight electricity, but the household’s unusually high daytime office consumption reduces its value: daytime electricity cannot normally be displaced by a battery discharged only during the evening peak.

Under the illustrative assumptions below, **neither a roughly 5 kWh nor 10 kWh battery pays for itself within 10 years**. The 5 kWh option is financially less poor because the 10 kWh battery is unlikely to use its extra capacity regularly. Backup power may justify a battery for resilience, but it should be treated as a separate non-financial benefit.

## Evidence and tariff availability

Octopus advertises Intelligent Octopus Go with six hours, 23:30–05:30, at 8p/kWh; its page also gives a July 2026 average price-cap unit rate of 26.11p/kWh, excluding standing charges and varying by region [S13]. However, Intelligent Octopus Go is principally an EV tariff. Eligibility and whether it permits economically advantageous charging of a battery without an eligible EV or charger must be confirmed in writing.

Octopus Go is also primarily designed for EV charging and may require an electric car, while its webpage does not disclose all relevant battery-compatible rates [S14]. Flux and Intelligent Flux are explicitly designed for customers with solar and batteries and therefore are not suitable evidence for a no-solar installation [S16][S17]. Agile is available for flexible users, but its half-hourly prices are volatile and it is most suitable where demand or batteries can be automated [S18].

Saving Sessions can occasionally provide free or discounted electricity without a battery, but sessions are uncertain and mainly benefit genuinely flexible appliances. They are less valuable here because much office consumption already occurs during the daytime session period [S20]. Export tariffs are irrelevant unless the system exports electricity; cited solar-and-battery payback claims must not be transferred to a battery-only installation [S15][S19].

## Calculation assumptions

The following is an indicative model, not a forecast or quotation:

- Annual use: 9,000 kWh; sensitivities at 6,000 and 11,000 kWh.
- Existing flat tariff: 24.7p/kWh.
- Illustrative ToU tariff:
  - 9p/kWh overnight;
  - 24.5p/kWh daytime/shoulder;
  - 30p/kWh peak.
- Consumption profile: 60% daytime, 20% peak/evening and 20% overnight. This reflects substantial daytime office use but assumes some evening and overnight demand.
- Standing charges are excluded because they are assumed equal. Actual tariff-specific standing charges must be included.
- Battery round-trip efficiency: 90%; delivering 1 kWh requires about 1.11 kWh of charging electricity.
- Usable capacity: 4.5 kWh from a nominal 5 kWh battery and 9 kWh from a nominal 10 kWh battery.
- The battery discharges only against otherwise expensive peak consumption.
- Annual delivered energy: about 1,200 kWh for the 5 kWh battery and 1,470 kWh for the 10 kWh battery. This assumes roughly 300 useful operating days, not a full cycle every day. It allows for some overnight consumption being used directly, insufficient peak demand, charging/discharging limits and periods when the price spread is unattractive.
- Battery savings fall by 2% annually for degradation; ten-year multiplier is approximately 9.15.
- No maintenance cost is included and no battery or inverter replacement is assumed within 10 years. This is optimistic.
- Installed prices: £3,200–£4,200 for 5 kWh and £4,600–£7,100 for 10 kWh; midpoints are £3,700 and £5,850 respectively [S12].
- No grant, export income or solar generation is assumed. Qualifying installations may receive 0% VAT until 2027, subject to HMRC conditions and the precise system configuration; this must be documented in the quotation [S12].

Costs below exclude standing charges and use the illustrative tariff rates.

| 1. Competitive flat tariff | £2,223 | — | £0 | — |
| 2. ToU, no battery | £2,025 | £198 | £0 | — |
| 3. 5 kWh battery + ToU | £1,785 | £438 | £3,200–£4,200 | £240 |
| 4. 10 kWh battery + ToU | £1,731 | £492 | £4,600–£7,100 | £294 |

The battery options’ total savings include approximately £198 per year from changing tariff. The battery itself contributes only about £240 or £294 annually relative to using the ToU tariff without a battery.

Simple battery payback is therefore approximately:

- **5 kWh:** 13–18 years;
- **10 kWh:** 16–24 years.

At midpoint prices, ten-year outcomes after degradation are approximately:

- 5 kWh: £240 × 9.15 = £2,196; less £3,700 installation = **–£1,500**.
- 10 kWh: £294 × 9.15 = £2,690; less £5,850 installation = **–£3,160**.

These figures exclude finance, replacement and maintenance, so actual outcomes could be worse.

## Key sensitivities

The base case provides roughly 20p/kWh of gross value per battery-delivered kWh: 30p peak electricity avoided, less approximately 10p effective charging cost after losses.

| Effective price spread | 5 kWh annual saving | 10 kWh annual saving |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 10p/kWh | £107 | £131 |
| 20p/kWh | £240 | £294 |
| 30p/kWh | £347 | £425 |

Even at a sustained 30p spread, midpoint simple payback is roughly 11 years for 5 kWh and 14 years for 10 kWh. Ten-year battery savings at that spread are about £3,175 and £3,889 respectively—still below the midpoint installation costs.

At only 10% of consumption during peak hours, savings fall to approximately £120 and £180 annually. At 30% peak consumption they rise to about £240 and £360, but the 5 kWh unit becomes capacity-limited and the 10 kWh unit still requires sufficient evening demand and suitable power ratings. A battery becomes more attractive if peak demand is materially higher, the battery cycles close to daily, the spread is large and durable, or installed costs are substantially below the assumed range.

The maximum capital cost consistent with a ten-year payback in the base case is only about £2,200 for the 5 kWh system and £2,700 for the 10 kWh system—before allowing for replacement. That is below the assumed installed prices.

## Recommendation

1. Obtain at least 12 months of half-hourly smart-meter data and calculate actual costs under competing flat and ToU tariffs, including standing charges.
2. Switch to a ToU tariff without a battery if the supplier confirms eligibility and the measured saving remains positive. This is likely to provide meaningful but modest savings, especially from naturally occurring overnight use; it is not guaranteed if peak/day rates exceed the current flat tariff.
3. Consider a **5 kWh battery** only with a low quotation, automatic tariff control, frequent measured evening demand and strong warranty terms.
4. Do not choose 10 kWh merely because consumption is high. Choose it only if interval data shows that its additional usable capacity can regularly discharge during expensive periods.

The strongest argument against this recommendation is that future wholesale volatility could produce much larger Agile spreads, allowing an automated battery to earn more than this conservative model predicts. The opposite risks are narrowing spreads, tariff restrictions, software failure, degradation, inverter replacement and changes to supplier terms.

Before deciding, obtain half-hourly demand data; postcode-specific tariff rates and standing charges; written confirmation that grid charging is permitted; at least three itemised quotations; usable rather than nominal capacity; continuous and peak charge/discharge power; round-trip efficiency; warranty and throughput limits; capacity-retention guarantee; reserve settings; backup equipment and outage operation; maintenance/software fees; VAT treatment; DNO requirements; and battery/inverter replacement costs.
