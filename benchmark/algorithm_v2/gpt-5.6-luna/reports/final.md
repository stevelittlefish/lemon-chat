# Is a Home Battery Worthwhile Without Solar Panels in the UK?

## Executive summary

For the specified English household, a home battery **can technically operate without solar panels**, charging from the grid during cheap periods and discharging when electricity is more expensive. Suppliers and manufacturers confirm that this operating model is possible, provided the tariff, battery software and installation permit it [S13][S24][S34][S44].

However, the financial case is less attractive than many solar-and-battery advertisements suggest. The household uses approximately **9,000 kWh a year**, but much of its demand occurs during daytime home-office hours. That is important because a battery creates the greatest value when it can replace electricity bought during expensive peak periods. Electricity consumed directly during the daytime may already be purchased at a relatively ordinary rate, and cannot necessarily be shifted through a battery.

Using an illustrative but realistic model for 14 July 2026:

- Remaining on a competitive flat tariff costs about **£2,223 a year**, excluding standing charges.
- Moving to a suitable time-of-use tariff without a battery costs about **£2,025 a year**, saving approximately **£198 annually**.
- A 5 kWh battery reduces the modelled bill to approximately **£1,785**, but the battery adds only about **£240 a year** of saving compared with the tariff alone.
- A 10 kWh battery reduces the modelled bill to approximately **£1,731**, adding only about **£294 a year** over the tariff-only option.

At indicative installed prices of approximately **£3,700 for 5 kWh** and **£5,850 for 10 kWh**, neither system is likely to recover its cost within ten years under the base assumptions. The 5 kWh system is financially closer, but still produces an estimated **£1,500 loss over ten years compared with simply switching tariff**. The 10 kWh system produces an estimated **£3,160 loss against the tariff-only alternative**.

The strongest financial recommendation is therefore:

> **Switch to a suitable time-of-use tariff first, without buying a battery. Consider a battery only if half-hourly data shows substantial evening peak demand, the tariff spread is unusually large and durable, and the installed quotation is substantially below ordinary market levels.**

Backup power, energy independence and future flexibility may justify a battery for some households, but those are separate benefits and should not be disguised as electricity-bill savings.

## Scope and methodology

This assessment considers an owner-occupied home in England as at **14 July 2026**, with no solar panels expected during the ten-year assessment period. The household has a smart meter and can change tariff, but it can manually shift only a small amount of consumption.

The analysis compares four choices:

1. A competitive flat-rate tariff without a battery.
2. A suitable time-of-use tariff without a battery.
3. A roughly 5 kWh battery on a time-of-use tariff.
4. A roughly 10 kWh battery on a time-of-use tariff.

The current flat-rate benchmark is **24.7p/kWh**, supplied in the question. At 9,000 kWh per year, the annual usage charge is:

\[
9,000 \times £0.247 = £2,223
\]

Standing charges are excluded from the main comparison because the model assumes they are broadly similar. In reality, they must be included in a supplier-specific quote. A July 2026 price-cap benchmark of approximately **26.11p/kWh** has also been reported, although actual regional rates and standing charges vary [S1][S30].

The illustrative time-of-use tariff uses:

- **9p/kWh overnight**
- **24.5p/kWh daytime or shoulder period**
- **30p/kWh during the expensive peak period**

These figures are modelling assumptions rather than a claim that every household can obtain them. Advertised overnight rates have included figures around 7–9p/kWh, while other tariffs and time windows may be materially more expensive [S13][S23][S48]. Some apparently attractive tariffs are designed principally for electric vehicles or require a compatible charger, so eligibility for a battery-only household must be confirmed in writing [S14][S21][S22].

The household’s assumed demand profile is:

- 60% daytime office and general daytime demand
- 20% evening or peak-period demand
- 20% overnight demand

For the no-battery time-of-use option, a modest amount of direct manual shifting is assumed. This produces an effective average unit cost of approximately **22.5p/kWh**, or:

\[
9,000 \times £0.225 = £2,025
\]

The battery calculations assume:

- 90% round-trip efficiency
- 5 kWh system with approximately 4.5 kWh usable capacity
- 10 kWh system with approximately 9 kWh usable capacity
- Approximately 300 useful operating days per year
- No assumption of a complete cycle every day
- Annual battery savings declining by 2% because of degradation and reduced performance
- No maintenance or replacement in the base case
- Midpoint installed prices of £3,700 and £5,850

A battery’s value is calculated from the electricity price avoided when it discharges, minus the cost of charging, conversion losses, under-utilisation and degradation. A nominal 10 kWh battery is not equivalent to 10 kWh of delivered household electricity: usable capacity, reserve settings, inverter limits and efficiency all matter.

## Option 1: Competitive flat-rate tariff without a battery

### Strengths

The flat-rate option is the simplest and most predictable. It requires no capital investment, no battery-management software, no tariff automation and no concern about battery degradation. The household can continue using electricity whenever it is needed, including during daytime office hours, without being penalised by a particularly high peak rate.

At 24.7p/kWh, the modelled annual usage cost is **£2,223**. This is a useful benchmark rather than necessarily the cheapest available outcome. A competitive flat tariff could outperform an unsuitable ToU tariff if the household consumes very little electricity overnight and cannot avoid expensive peak periods.

The option also avoids the risk that a supplier changes tariff eligibility, changes its cheap-period window or withdraws automated battery support. Agile tariffs, for example, vary hourly and are best suited to customers who can shift substantial consumption or automate it reliably [S7][S18].

### Weaknesses

The flat tariff gives up the opportunity to use cheap overnight electricity directly. Even though the home office uses much of its electricity during the day, some appliances, laundry, dishwashing, water heating or other discretionary loads may be capable of moving overnight.

The household also pays the same rate for electricity that could potentially be purchased at 9p/kWh. A battery is not necessary to capture the value of loads that can be shifted directly. This is why the tariff-only option deserves consideration before any equipment purchase.

### Ideal use case

A flat tariff is ideal where:

- Overnight consumption is very small.
- Peak-period consumption is unavoidable.
- The available ToU tariff has a punitive daytime or evening rate.
- The household values simplicity and price certainty more than potential savings.
- The household is not eligible for the apparently attractive EV-linked tariffs.

It is a sensible fallback, but not the strongest financial choice under the base assumptions.

## Option 2: Suitable time-of-use tariff without a battery

### Strengths

This is the strongest option financially in the base case because it captures savings without requiring capital expenditure. The household can use cheap overnight electricity directly for any naturally flexible consumption. Even limited manual shifting can produce a meaningful reduction.

The illustrative annual bill falls from **£2,223 to £2,025**, a saving of approximately **£198 a year**. Over ten years, without assuming tariff changes, that is approximately **£1,980** of cumulative saving.

This option also avoids round-trip losses. If a washing machine runs directly at 9p/kWh, all the energy is purchased at 9p/kWh. If a battery is charged at 9p/kWh and later discharges, some energy is lost in the battery and inverter. At 90% round-trip efficiency, every 1 kWh delivered later requires approximately 1.11 kWh of charging energy.

The household’s high daytime office demand is not necessarily a disadvantage for tariff-only switching. If daytime rates are close to the flat-rate benchmark, office electricity can continue to be consumed directly, while only genuinely flexible loads are moved overnight. A battery, by contrast, may have limited value if much of the daytime demand is already bought at a moderate shoulder rate.

### Weaknesses

A ToU tariff without a battery provides little benefit for inflexible consumption. The home office uses 10–20 kWh per day, much of it during working hours. That demand is difficult to move to the overnight period without changing working patterns or installing a battery large and powerful enough to serve it.

The result is highly tariff-specific. The overnight price may be attractive, but the peak rate, peak hours, standing charge and eligibility conditions can eliminate the saving. Octopus confirms that Agile is volatile and more suitable for customers able to shift usage or automate loads [S18]. Several supplier tariffs marketed around cheap overnight rates are designed principally for EVs or require a compatible EV charger [S13][S14][S21].

### Ideal use case

This is the best option where:

- The tariff is genuinely available to a battery-free household.
- The overnight period is long enough for direct loads to fit within it.
- Peak prices are not excessively high.
- The household can move at least some discretionary consumption.
- The homeowner wants savings without equipment risk.

It should normally be tested first for at least several months, using actual half-hourly consumption and bills.

## Option 3: Approximately 5 kWh battery plus a ToU tariff

### Strengths

A 5 kWh battery is more likely than a 10 kWh system to achieve reasonable utilisation in a home without solar. It can charge overnight and discharge into the evening or other expensive periods. A smaller system also has a lower installed price and lower risk of buying capacity that is rarely used.

Assuming 4.5 kWh usable capacity, 300 useful cycles per year and 90% efficiency, the model estimates approximately **1,200 kWh of delivered battery energy annually**. At the assumed spread, this produces about **£240 of annual saving over the ToU tariff**.

The 5 kWh option may be appropriate where half-hourly data shows that the household regularly has 3–4.5 kWh of demand during the tariff’s expensive period. It could also provide limited emergency power, although backup normally requires a gateway, protected circuits and a reserve setting. A manufacturer’s whole-home backup feature should not be assumed to be included in the advertised battery price [S47].

### Weaknesses

The battery-specific saving is only £240 a year in the base model. At an installed midpoint cost of £3,700, simple payback is:

\[
£3,700 \div £240 \approx 15.4 \text{ years}
\]

That is longer than many battery warranties and longer than the period before some owners may face inverter or battery replacement. Ten-year warranties commonly contain capacity-retention, throughput or cycle limits rather than guaranteeing unrestricted original performance [S55].

The 5 kWh system may also be unable to serve the home office adequately. If the office consumes 2–3 kW continuously, the battery’s maximum discharge power becomes as important as its capacity. A 5 kWh battery with a 2.5 kW inverter may not cover the full office load, and it may run out before the highest-value evening period.

### Ideal use case

The 5 kWh system is the more defensible battery choice where:

- A no-solar battery-only tariff is confirmed in writing.
- Peak demand is regular and measurable.
- The system can discharge at the required continuous power.
- The installed price is close to **£2,200 or less** for the base-case economics.
- The owner values backup as well as savings.
- The battery can be operated for substantially more than 300 high-value days each year.

At ordinary prices, however, it is not likely to pay for itself against the ToU tariff.

## Option 4: Approximately 10 kWh battery plus a ToU tariff

### Strengths

A 10 kWh battery can store more overnight electricity and may serve a longer evening or daytime period. The model assumes approximately 9 kWh usable capacity and around **1,470 kWh of delivered energy annually**, producing approximately **£294 of battery-specific saving**.

This option becomes more attractive if the home has substantial peak demand extending beyond a single evening period. It may also provide better backup duration. For a household that experiences frequent outages, a 10 kWh system can supply more essential loads, subject to inverter power and backup configuration.

### Weaknesses

The additional capacity has surprisingly little incremental value in the base case. The 10 kWh system adds only about **£54 a year** of saving compared with the 5 kWh system:

\[
£294 - £240 = £54
\]

That is because the household’s demand is concentrated during daytime working hours, while some overnight electricity is consumed directly and does not need to pass through the battery. The larger battery therefore cannot automatically create a proportionally larger saving.

At a midpoint installed price of £5,850, simple payback is approximately:

\[
£5,850 \div £294 \approx 19.9 \text{ years}
\]

The ten-year outcome is approximately **£1,180 worse than remaining on the flat-rate benchmark**, and approximately **£3,160 worse than choosing the ToU tariff without a battery**.

### Ideal use case

A 10 kWh system is appropriate only where:

- The household regularly consumes at least 7–9 kWh during high-price periods.
- The battery and inverter can sustain the required output.
- The spread between charging and discharging prices is unusually large.
- The system can cycle almost daily without being curtailed by tariff rules.
- The quotation is substantially below the illustrative market range.
- Backup duration is a major objective.

For this particular household, annual consumption alone is not sufficient justification for 10 kWh.

## Shared Considerations

### Tariff eligibility and market rules

The battery’s financial value depends principally on the import tariff, not on the battery in isolation. Some tariffs explicitly require solar, such as Flux products designed around solar and batteries [S16][S17][S25]. Those tariffs should not be assumed to be available to a grid-charged battery-only home.

Other tariffs are marketed for EVs and may require a compatible car or charger [S13][S14][S21]. The homeowner must obtain written confirmation that:

- A standalone battery is eligible.
- Grid charging is permitted.
- Automated charging and discharge are permitted.
- The supplier will not penalise or restrict battery arbitrage.
- Export is allowed, if relevant.
- The battery-control software remains supported.

Agile tariffs can offer larger spreads but expose the customer to volatile prices. A fixed overnight tariff may provide less upside but greater predictability.

### Usable capacity, power and efficiency

Nominal capacity is not the same as usable capacity. A nominal 5 kWh battery may have only 4.5 kWh available after reserve settings and manufacturer limits. The usable amount may decline further with age.

Maximum power is equally important. A 10 kWh battery discharging at 2 kW takes five hours to empty, while one discharging at 5 kW can serve a much larger instantaneous load. The homeowner should obtain:

- Continuous charge power.
- Continuous discharge power.
- Short-term peak power.
- Usable capacity at installation.
- Minimum reserve percentage.
- Round-trip efficiency under realistic operating conditions.
- Whether output falls at low state of charge or low temperature.

Manufacturers’ efficiency claims often apply under particular test conditions. Marketing figures should not be treated as measured household performance without technical documentation.

### Cycling assumptions

A full cycle every day must not be assumed. The household’s high daytime usage and existing overnight demand mean that a battery may not always have enough high-value discharge demand.

The base model uses about 300 useful days per year. Some days may have little evening consumption; on other days, the battery may be prevented from charging fully because the cheap period is too short or because the tariff-control software does not respond as expected. Conversely, a household with substantial evening demand and a wide price spread could achieve more than 300 valuable cycles.

### Degradation and warranties

The base case assumes battery savings fall by 2% annually. That is not a universal degradation forecast, but it reflects the fact that usable capacity and efficiency may decline.

Warranty terms require close inspection. A “10-year warranty” may be conditional on:

- A maximum throughput.
- A maximum number of cycles.
- A minimum end-of-warranty capacity, such as 70% or 80%.
- Use with an approved inverter or tariff-control platform.
- Registration and software connectivity.

A replacement inverter or battery during the ten-year period could materially worsen the economics. No replacement is included in the base case, so the results are relatively favourable to purchasing.

### VAT, grants and export

No battery grant is assumed. The Boiler Upgrade Scheme concerns heat pumps and biomass boilers, not standalone batteries [S39]. The Feed-in Tariff closed to new applications in 2019 and cannot be treated as revenue for a new battery-only installation [S52].

HMRC’s VAT treatment is important. Professionally supplied and installed qualifying energy-saving materials may benefit from 0% VAT under the relevant relief on 14 July 2026, although the rules and scheduled rate changes must be checked carefully [S26]. A separately purchased battery may be standard-rated, and quotations should clearly state VAT.

SEG payments are primarily for eligible renewable generation. Solar export rates should not be included in a battery-only calculation [S37][S49][S51]. A grid-charged battery should not be assumed to earn export income unless the supplier explicitly confirms eligibility and the arrangement is legal and economic.

Higher consumption improves the case only if the additional electricity occurs during periods the battery can serve. A household using 11,000 kWh mostly during cheap or moderate daytime periods may gain less than a 6,000 kWh household with concentrated 4–7pm demand.

### Price-spread sensitivity

The following estimates show approximate annual battery savings at different effective spreads after considering the modelled utilisation pattern:

| Effective charging-to-discharge spread | 5 kWh | 10 kWh |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 10p/kWh | £107/year | £131/year |
| 20p/kWh | £240/year | £294/year |
| 30p/kWh | £347/year | £425/year |

The headline difference between 9p overnight and 30p peak is 21p/kWh, but losses reduce the effective value. A 30p spread would improve the case, but even then the battery may not pay back if it is under-used or expensive to install.

### Peak-demand sensitivity

If only 10% of annual consumption occurs in battery-serviceable peak periods, estimated savings may fall to approximately **£120 for 5 kWh** and **£180 for 10 kWh** annually. If 30% occurs during those periods, savings may rise to approximately **£240 and £360**, subject to power and usable-capacity limits.

This is the central reason the home office matters. High annual consumption is not enough. The critical measurement is:

> How many kWh are consumed during the periods when the battery would discharge, and at what instantaneous power?

### Installation-cost sensitivity

At the base utilisation and price spread, ten-year payback against the ToU-only option would require an approximate installed price of:

- **£2,200 or less for 5 kWh**
- **£2,700 or less for 10 kWh**

Those thresholds are before unexpected replacement costs. A 3–4% annual degradation rate, software fees, restricted cycling, inverter replacement or extra electrical work would lower the acceptable purchase price further.

## Best For verdicts

**Best for lowest-risk savings: Option 2, ToU tariff without a battery**, because it can save approximately £198 a year without capital expenditure, degradation or replacement risk.

**Best for simplicity and certainty: Option 1, competitive flat tariff**, if the household cannot access a genuinely battery-compatible ToU tariff or has very little overnight/flexible demand.

**Best battery choice if buying is unavoidable: Option 3, approximately 5 kWh**, because it has a lower capital cost and is less likely to be substantially under-used. It should be purchased only with strong half-hourly evidence of regular peak demand and an unusually competitive quotation.

**Best for backup rather than bill savings: Option 4, approximately 10 kWh**, provided the owner values longer outage protection and accepts that backup is unlikely to pay for itself through tariff arbitrage alone.

**Worst financial choice under the base assumptions: Option 4**, because the extra 5 kWh adds only about £54 of annual saving while adding approximately £2,150 to the midpoint capital cost.

## What could change the conclusion?

The conclusion could change if the homeowner obtains a durable effective spread of at least **25–30p/kWh**, can cycle the battery on nearly every day, has materially more evening peak demand than assumed, or receives a much lower installed quotation.

A battery could also become more valuable if automated flexibility-market payments become substantial. Government policy recognises storage and demand flexibility as part of the future electricity system [S27][S28], but future payments are uncertain and should not be included without a firm, contractually documented revenue stream.

The conclusion could become less favourable if tariffs narrow, cheap periods shorten, battery software becomes incompatible, the battery degrades faster than assumed, or an inverter requires replacement.

## Information to obtain before purchasing

The homeowner should first download at least **12 months of half-hourly smart-meter data**. The analysis should identify:

- Consumption overnight.
- Consumption during each proposed peak period.
- The home office’s actual power demand.
- Maximum simultaneous load.
- Days on which the battery could realistically complete a valuable cycle.
- Electricity already consumed directly during cheap periods.

They should then obtain postcode-specific tariff quotations showing:

- Every unit rate and time window.
- Standing charge.
- Eligibility requirements.
- Exit fees and price-change terms.
- Whether standalone batteries are permitted.
- Whether automated battery control is supported.
- Export rules and any restrictions on grid-charged electricity.

At least three itemised battery quotations should specify:

- Battery model and usable capacity.
- Inverter model.
- Continuous and peak charge/discharge power.
- Round-trip efficiency.
- Reserve setting.
- Warranty duration, throughput and end-of-warranty capacity.
- Expected degradation.
- Backup gateway and protected circuits.
- Installation, cabling and DNO work.
- Monitoring or software fees.
- Maintenance.
- Replacement cost assumptions.
- VAT treatment.

## Conclusion

For this English household, a home battery without solar is **technically viable but not normally financially worthwhile at current indicative prices**.

The 5 kWh battery is the more plausible size, but its expected payback is around 15 years under the base assumptions and its ten-year outcome is negative compared with simply changing tariff. The 10 kWh battery is even less attractive financially because the household’s daytime-heavy demand does not create enough additional high-value discharge opportunity.

Changing to a suitable time-of-use tariff without buying a battery is likely to provide meaningful savings—approximately **£198 a year in the illustrative model**—and is the best first step. It captures direct overnight savings while avoiding capital cost, losses, degradation and tariff-control risk.

The strongest argument against this recommendation is that a future tariff with very large spreads, reliable automation and flexibility payments could make a battery more valuable than the conservative model suggests. But the strongest argument against buying now is more immediate: much of the household’s electricity is consumed directly during daytime office hours, while a battery can only save money on the peak demand it can physically and economically replace.

**Recommendation:** obtain half-hourly data and confirmed tariff quotations, switch tariff first if the numbers work, and buy a battery only if measured peak demand, battery power, usable capacity, price spread and installed cost together demonstrate a realistic ten-year return.
