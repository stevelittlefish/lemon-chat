# Updated assessment: home battery without solar in England

## Bottom line

On the stated facts, a time-of-use (ToU) tariff without a battery is the financially safest first step. A battery can technically charge from the grid overnight and discharge during expensive periods, but this household’s unusually high daytime office demand weakens the economics: electricity used directly during the day cannot be displaced by stored electricity, while the battery still incurs efficiency losses, degradation and capital cost [S3][S5].

Under the illustrative assumptions below, neither a 5 kWh nor a 10 kWh battery pays for itself within 10 years. The 10 kWh system is not automatically better: the household has insufficient evening/peak demand to use its additional capacity regularly. The conclusion could change with unusually low installation cost, a sustained and very large peak/off-peak price spread, or materially more peak-time consumption.

## Assumptions

The calculation is indicative rather than a quotation or forecast. Exact 14 July 2026 tariff prices were not supplied by the available sources; Agile prices vary hourly and savings depend on the ability to shift demand [S7]. I assume:

- Annual consumption: 9,000 kWh; 6,000 and 11,000 kWh are tested below.
- Existing flat tariff: 24.7p/kWh, giving £2,223 annually before standing charge.
- Competitive ToU tariff:
  - 9p/kWh overnight;
  - 24.5p/kWh daytime/shoulder;
  - 30p/kWh peak.
- Demand profile: 60% daytime, 20% peak/evening, 20% overnight. This reflects substantial office consumption but assumes some evening and overnight load.
- Standing charges are equal and therefore excluded. They must be included in a real comparison.
- The battery charges during the cheap period and discharges only against otherwise expensive peak demand.
- Round-trip efficiency: 90%; therefore, each 1 kWh delivered requires approximately 1.11 kWh imported for charging. Published commercial guidance commonly quotes roughly 90–98%, but the quotation’s guaranteed figure matters [S9][S12].
- Indicative usable capacities: 4.5 kWh for a nominal 5 kWh battery and 9 kWh for a nominal 10 kWh battery.
- Maximum power is assumed adequate for the relevant load. Actual continuous charge/discharge power could restrict savings, particularly where several office or household appliances operate simultaneously.
- The 5 kWh battery delivers about 1,200 kWh annually to peak loads: approximately 4 kWh on 300 days. The 10 kWh battery delivers about 1,470 kWh: it is limited by the household’s approximately 1,800 kWh annual peak demand, not by its nominal capacity. This does **not** assume a full daily cycle.
- Battery savings decline by 2% annually for degradation. No maintenance cost is assumed, but no replacement is included within the 10-year period. This is optimistic if the warranty does not cover the required throughput or if an inverter must be replaced.
- Indicative installed prices:
  - 5 kWh: £3,200–£4,200; midpoint £3,700.
  - 10 kWh: £4,600–£7,100; midpoint £5,850 [S12].
- A qualifying installation may benefit from 0% VAT until 2027, subject to the precise HMRC rules and configuration; this must be confirmed in the quotation [S12]. There is no assumed grant, export income or solar generation.

## Annual comparison at 9,000 kWh

| Option | Indicative annual import cost before standing charge | Saving versus current flat tariff | Battery capital cost | Simple payback |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1. Competitive flat tariff | £2,223 | — | — | — |
| 2. ToU tariff, no battery | £2,025 | £198 | — | Immediate |
| 3. 5 kWh battery + ToU | £1,785 | £438 total; £240 attributable to battery | £3,200–£4,200 | 13–18 years on battery saving |
| 4. 10 kWh battery + ToU | £1,731 | £492 total; £294 attributable to battery | £4,600–£7,100 | 16–24 years on battery saving |

The battery-option costs include the ToU tariff benefit and subtract the cost of charging energy from the value of peak electricity displaced. They do not imply that the battery saves £438 or £492 every year independently: approximately £198 is available simply by changing tariff under this demand profile.

At the midpoint installation prices and 2% annual degradation, ten-year battery savings are approximately:

- 5 kWh: £240 × 9.15 = £2,196; net after £3,700 installation: **–£1,500**.
- 10 kWh: £294 × 9.15 = £2,690; net after £5,850 installation: **–£3,160**.

These are before financing costs and possible replacement, but also before any value assigned to backup power. Backup may justify expenditure for resilience, but it is not a financial saving.

## Usage sensitivity

Using the same demand proportions and price assumptions:

| Annual use | Flat tariff | ToU, no battery | ToU + 5 kWh | ToU + 10 kWh |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 6,000 kWh | £1,482 | £1,350 | about £1,150 | about £1,154 |
| 9,000 kWh | £2,223 | £2,025 | about £1,785 | about £1,731 |
| 11,000 kWh | £2,717 | £2,475 | about £2,182 | about £2,116 |

Approximate annual battery-attributable savings are £160/£196 at 6,000 kWh, £240/£294 at 9,000 kWh, and £293/£359 at 11,000 kWh for the 5/10 kWh systems respectively. At the midpoint prices, corresponding ten-year net outcomes are approximately:

- 5 kWh: **–£2,236, –£1,500, –£1,021**.
- 10 kWh: **–£4,057, –£3,160, –£2,565**.

Higher consumption helps only if it occurs during periods the battery can serve. Extra daytime office consumption generally improves neither battery utilisation nor arbitrage.

## Sensitivity to price spread

The base case gives approximately 20p/kWh of value per battery-delivered kWh: 30p peak less roughly 10p effective charging cost after 90% efficiency. Approximate annual battery savings are:

| Effective peak/off-peak spread | 5 kWh battery | 10 kWh battery |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 10p/kWh | £107 | £131 |
| 20p/kWh (base case) | £240 | £294 |
| 30p/kWh | £347 | £425 |

At a sustained 30p spread, the 5 kWh midpoint payback is still about 11 years and the 10 kWh payback about 14 years, before replacement. A battery-only investment therefore requires an unusually favourable and durable spread, very low installation cost, or substantially more annual cycling to become compelling.

## Sensitivity to peak demand and cycling

If only 10% of annual consumption occurs during expensive peak periods, indicative annual battery savings fall to roughly £120 for the 5 kWh system and £180 for the 10 kWh system. At 30% peak consumption, they rise only to about £240 and £360 respectively in this model, because the 5 kWh system is then constrained by its usable capacity and assumed cycling, while the 10 kWh system remains constrained by realistic peak demand and losses.

This is why nominal capacity is a poor basis for the decision. The quotation must specify usable kWh, continuous and maximum charge/discharge power, reserve settings, round-trip efficiency, permitted throughput and end-of-warranty capacity. A 10 kWh battery that frequently sits idle is worse value than a smaller unit that cycles reliably.

## Tariff and regulatory uncertainty

A ToU tariff can provide meaningful savings without a battery if the household has enough naturally occurring overnight demand or can schedule appliances. However, this household’s office load is mainly daytime, and only limited consumption can be shifted manually. Some tariffs may therefore make the bill worse if their daytime or peak rate exceeds the current flat rate. Octopus identifies Agile and Tracker as possible smart-tariff options, but Agile prices vary hourly and are most suitable for customers able to shift consumption [S7]. Commercial guides quote examples such as approximately 9p overnight and 24p elsewhere, but these are not guaranteed current prices and should not be treated as forecasts [S8].

Standing charges, eligibility, exit fees, automated-control requirements and tariff changes must be included. Export tariffs are largely irrelevant without solar, and no export income should be assumed. The installer should confirm any DNO notification or approval requirements, metering configuration, electrical protection and whether the proposed system can operate safely during a power cut. VAT treatment should be documented rather than assumed.

## Recommendation

1. Obtain 12 months of half-hourly smart-meter data and first compare the actual weighted cost of a competitive flat tariff with confirmed ToU tariffs.
2. Switch tariff without buying a battery if the calculated annual saving remains positive after standing charges and the peak rate is acceptable. This is likely to be the best financial option.
3. Consider a 5 kWh battery only if a quotation is near the low end of the range, the system can automatically charge and discharge, and measured data shows frequent evening peak demand.
4. Choose 10 kWh only if the data demonstrates regular high peak consumption and the additional usable capacity produces enough extra discharge to justify its higher price. On the present assumptions, it does not.

The strongest argument against this recommendation is that future wholesale-price volatility could create much larger Agile spreads than assumed, allowing a correctly controlled battery to earn more than the model predicts. Conversely, future tariff redesign, supplier restrictions, software failure, degradation, inverter replacement or a narrowing spread could make the investment substantially worse.

Before purchasing, obtain: half-hourly demand data; full tariff schedules and standing charges; at least three itemised installed quotations; usable capacity; charge/discharge limits; efficiency; warranty duration and throughput; degradation guarantee; reserve and backup capability; maintenance and software fees; replacement pricing; VAT treatment; DNO requirements; and confirmation that the battery can automatically follow the selected tariff.
