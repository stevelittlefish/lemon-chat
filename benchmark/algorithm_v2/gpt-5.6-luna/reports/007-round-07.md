# Updated assessment: home battery without solar in England

## Bottom line

For this household, **switching to a suitable time-of-use (ToU) tariff without buying a battery is the strongest financial option**. On the illustrative assumptions below, it saves about **£198 a year** with no capital cost, degradation or tariff-lock-in risk.

A grid-charged battery can operate without solar: it charges during cheap periods and discharges when electricity is expensive [S44][S45][S50]. However, the household’s unusually high daytime office demand reduces the value of storage. Electricity used directly during working hours cannot be shifted by a battery unless the battery is large enough, powerful enough and allowed to discharge throughout the daytime.

Neither a 5 kWh nor a 10 kWh system is likely to pay for itself against the best available ToU alternative at ordinary installed prices. A 5 kWh battery is the less risky option, but remains financially marginal. A 10 kWh battery is unlikely to be worthwhile solely for bill savings.

## Evidence and tariff assumptions

The calculation uses:

- Annual consumption: **9,000 kWh**
- Existing flat rate: **24.7p/kWh**
- Gas heating; no meaningful electric-heating load
- Illustrative ToU rates: **9p/kWh overnight, 24.5p/kWh daytime/shoulder, 30p/kWh peak**
- Equal standing charges across tariffs, therefore excluded
- Approximately 60% of demand in daytime office hours, 20% in evening/peak periods and 20% overnight
- A small amount of manual shifting is assumed for the battery-free ToU case, producing an effective average price of about 22.5p/kWh
- Battery round-trip efficiency: **90%**
- 5 kWh system: **4.5 kWh usable capacity**
- 10 kWh system: **9 kWh usable capacity**
- Approximately 300 useful operating days per year, rather than a full cycle every day
- Annual delivered battery energy: approximately **1,200 kWh for 5 kWh** and **1,470 kWh for 10 kWh**
- Battery savings decline by **2% annually**
- Midpoint installed prices: **£3,700 for 5 kWh** and **£5,850 for 10 kWh**
- No maintenance or replacement during the base-case ten years

These are modelling assumptions, not quotations. A July 2026 capped average rate has been reported at 26.11p/kWh, while advertised overnight rates can be about 7–9p/kWh or, on some tariffs, around 13p/kWh windows [S13][S21][S48][S54]. Some products and tariffs are principally designed for electric vehicles and may require a compatible vehicle or charger [S21][S22]. Battery-only eligibility must be confirmed directly with the supplier.

The tariff must explicitly permit standalone grid charging, automated discharge and any export. Agile tariffs may offer larger spreads but expose the household to volatile prices and require reliable control [S18]. Future tariff eligibility, price spreads and software arrangements are uncertain.

## Indicative annual costs

| Option | Annual electricity cost | Saving versus existing flat tariff | Capital cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1. Competitive flat tariff | £2,223 | — | £0 |
| 2. ToU, no battery | £2,025 | £198 | £0 |
| 3. ToU + 5 kWh battery | £1,785 | £438 | £3,200–£4,200 |
| 4. ToU + 10 kWh battery | £1,731 | £492 | £4,600–£7,100 |

The battery-specific saving over the ToU tariff is only approximately:

- **5 kWh:** £240 per year
- **10 kWh:** £294 per year

The extra 5 kWh therefore adds only about £54 annually in this model. High annual consumption does not automatically make a large battery attractive: much of this home’s load occurs during the day and is already supplied directly from the grid.

The 21p nominal difference between 9p overnight and 30p peak is reduced by charging and discharging losses. The model uses an effective value of roughly 20p per delivered kWh before further limits from reserve capacity, power ratings and imperfect scheduling.

## Ten-year outcome

Applying a 2% annual decline in battery savings gives a ten-year multiplier of approximately 9.15.

| Option | Ten-year saving versus flat tariff | Capital cost | Net ten-year outcome |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Flat tariff | £0 | £0 | £0 |
| ToU only | £1,980 | £0 | **+£1,980** |
| ToU + 5 kWh battery | £4,176 | £3,700 midpoint | **+£476** |
| ToU + 10 kWh battery | £4,670 | £5,850 midpoint | **–£1,180** |

Compared specifically with switching to ToU without a battery:

- 5 kWh: £240 × 9.15 − £3,700 = approximately **–£1,500**
- 10 kWh: £294 × 9.15 − £5,850 = approximately **–£3,160**

Simple battery payback is approximately **13–18 years for 5 kWh** and **16–24 years for 10 kWh**, before replacements or financing. An inverter replacement, maintenance, software fees, electrical work or battery replacement would worsen those outcomes. Ten-year warranties commonly include capacity-retention or throughput limits rather than guaranteeing unrestricted performance [S55]. Backup operation may require a gateway, protected circuits and reserve capacity, which may not be included in the advertised price [S47][S56].

## Key sensitivities

Approximate annual battery savings at different effective overnight-to-peak spreads are:

| Effective spread | 5 kWh | 10 kWh |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 10p/kWh | £107 | £131 |
| 20p/kWh | £240 | £294 |
| 30p/kWh | £347 | £425 |

If only 10% of consumption occurs in battery-serviceable peak periods, annual savings may fall to roughly **£120 for 5 kWh** and **£180 for 10 kWh**. At 30% peak demand, they may rise to approximately **£240 and £360**, subject to usable capacity and inverter power.

At base utilisation, ten-year payback against ToU would require an installed price of roughly **£2,200 or less for 5 kWh** or **£2,700 or less for 10 kWh**, before replacement costs. A 3–4% annual degradation rate, lower usable capacity, restricted charge/discharge power or an inverter replacement would reduce those acceptable prices. Commercial price articles sometimes quote prices around £3,600 or £4,000, but these are starting prices or mixed equipment/installation figures and do not establish a property-specific payback [S50][S54][S55].

## Taxes, incentives and market rules

No battery grant is assumed. The Boiler Upgrade Scheme does not fund batteries [S39]. FIT payments are unavailable to new installations since the scheme closed to new applications in 2019 [S52]. SEG is primarily for eligible renewable generation and its solar-export figures do not apply to a battery charged solely from the grid [S37][S49][S51]. Any export arrangement must be checked with the supplier and network operator.

Qualifying professionally supplied energy-saving materials may receive 0% VAT under applicable HMRC conditions, with a scheduled change to 5% from April 2027; separately purchased equipment may be standard-rated [S26]. Quotations should state the VAT treatment.

## Recommendation

1. **Obtain and compare tariffs first.** A ToU tariff could save about £200 annually without any equipment, provided its cheap period matches naturally overnight consumption and its standing charge is not materially higher.
2. **Choose ToU without a battery as the default financial option.**
3. **Consider 5 kWh only** if interval data shows substantial evening/peak demand, the system can discharge at the required power, the supplier permits battery-only operation, and the quotation is unusually low.
4. **Do not choose 10 kWh solely because annual consumption is 9,000 kWh.** It needs frequent, high-value cycling to justify its larger capital cost.
5. A battery becomes more attractive if the price spread is durable and at least 25–30p/kWh effective, installation prices fall substantially, peak demand is much higher than assumed, or flexibility-market payments materially increase. Conversely, narrower spreads, tariff restrictions, faster degradation and replacement costs make it less attractive.

The strongest argument against this recommendation is that future volatile tariffs and flexibility markets could reward automated storage more than this conservative model [S40][S41][S42][S43]. The strongest argument against buying now is that daytime office electricity is consumed directly, while losses, finite power, degradation and changing tariff rules reduce the battery’s arbitrage value.

Before deciding, obtain 12 months of half-hourly smart-meter data; written flat and ToU quotations including standing charges and all eligibility rules; and at least three itemised no-solar battery quotations. Confirm usable capacity, continuous and peak charge/discharge power, efficiency, reserve settings, degradation and capacity-retention guarantees, throughput limits, backup hardware, outage performance, software fees, VAT, DNO requirements, maintenance and replacement costs. Specifically calculate what proportion of office demand falls inside each tariff’s peak period.
