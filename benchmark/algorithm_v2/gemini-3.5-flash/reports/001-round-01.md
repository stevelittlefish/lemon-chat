### Executive Summary

As of July 2026, installing a standalone home battery without solar panels in England is **not financially viable** for this household. While a battery enables substantial annual bill savings by exploiting the spread between off-peak and peak electricity tariffs, the high upfront capital costs of battery systems do not pay for themselves within their 10-year warranty periods. 

Conversely, switching to a time-of-use (ToU) tariff *without* a battery is also financially detrimental. Because the household has high, inflexible daytime demand from a home office and cannot manually shift consumption, the premium daytime rates of ToU tariffs would increase annual electricity bills compared to remaining on a competitive flat-rate tariff.

The optimal financial strategy is to **remain on a competitive flat-rate tariff** (currently £0.247/kWh) or seek a lower fixed flat-rate deal, while deferring a battery purchase until hardware and installation costs decline significantly.

*   **Home Office Demand:** 15 kWh/day (midpoint of 10–20 kWh range) consumed primarily during daytime working hours (08:00–18:00).
*   **Base Flat-Rate Tariff:** £0.247/kWh (user's current rate). *Note: The Ofgem standard variable price cap for July–September 2026 is £0.2611/kWh [S10], making the user's current rate highly competitive.*

    *   *Option B (Economy 7):* Off-peak rate of £0.1453/kWh (7-hour window) and a peak rate of £0.3161/kWh [S3].

### Comparison of the Four Options

#### Option 1: Remain on Flat-Rate Tariff (£0.247/kWh) without a Battery
*   **Annual Cost:** $9,000 \text{ kWh} \times £0.247 = £2,223.00$
*   **10-Year Cost:** £22,230.00
*   **Capital Cost:** £0
*   **Net Financial Outcome:** Baseline (£0 savings)

#### Option 2: Switch to ToU Tariff without a Battery
To save money on a ToU tariff without a battery, a household must shift 30% to 45% of its total consumption to the overnight window [S3, S6]. 
*   **Household Profile:** Out of 24.66 kWh daily usage, 15 kWh is locked into daytime home office hours. The remaining 9.66 kWh covers baseload, cooking, and evening use. Only a tiny fraction can be shifted manually.
*   **Estimated Split:** 85% peak (7,650 kWh at £0.28/kWh) and 15% off-peak (1,350 kWh baseload at £0.085/kWh).
*   **Annual Cost:** $(7,650 \times £0.28) + (1,350 \times £0.085) = £2,142.00 + £114.75 = £2,256.75$
*   **Annual Savings:** -£33.75 (An *increase* in cost compared to the flat rate).
*   **10-Year Net Outcome:** **-£337.50** (Loss)

#### Option 3: Install a ~5 kWh Battery & Switch to ToU Tariff
*   **Battery Specs:** 5 kWh nominal capacity, 90% usable (4.5 kWh), 90% round-trip efficiency, 10-year warranty. Max charge/discharge rate: 2.5 kW.
*   **Installed Cost:** £4,000 (including inverter and smart controls) [S11].
*   **Operation:** The battery charges fully overnight during the 5-hour off-peak window (taking 5 kWh from the grid to store 4.5 kWh usable due to efficiency losses). 
    *   *Overnight Direct Consumption:* During the 5-hour off-peak window, the home's baseload (estimated at 0.3 kW, or 1.5 kWh total) is consumed directly from the grid at the cheap £0.085/kWh rate, rather than passing through the battery.
    *   *Daytime Discharge:* The battery discharges its 4.5 kWh during peak hours, offsetting 4.5 kWh of daytime demand.
*   **Annual Arbitrage Balance:**
    *   Stored & Discharged: $4.5 \text{ kWh/day} \times 365 \text{ days} = 1,642.5 \text{ kWh/year}$ offset from peak.
    *   Grid Import for Storage (with 10% loss): $1,642.5 / 0.90 = 1,825 \text{ kWh/year}$ imported off-peak.
    *   Direct Off-Peak Consumption (Baseload): $1.5 \text{ kWh/day} \times 365 = 547.5 \text{ kWh/year}$.
    *   Remaining Peak Import: $9,000 - 1,642.5 - 547.5 = 6,810 \text{ kWh/year}$.
*   **Annual Cost:** 
    *   Peak Import: $6,810 \text{ kWh} \times £0.28 = £1,906.80$
    *   Off-Peak Import (Storage + Baseload): $(1,825 + 547.5) \times £0.085 = £201.66$
    *   Total: £2,108.46
*   **Annual Savings vs. Option 1:** $£2,223.00 - £2,108.46 = £114.54$
*   **Simple Payback:** $£4,000 / £114.54 = \mathbf{34.9 \text{ years}}$
*   **10-Year Net Financial Outcome:** $-£4,000 + (10 \times £114.54) = \mathbf{-£2,854.60}$ (Loss)

#### Option 4: Install a ~10 kWh Battery & Switch to ToU Tariff
*   **Battery Specs:** 10 kWh nominal, 90% usable (9.0 kWh), 90% round-trip efficiency, 10-year warranty. Max charge/discharge rate: 5.0 kW.
*   **Installed Cost:** £6,500 [S11].
*   **Operation:** The battery charges fully overnight (taking 10 kWh from the grid to store 9.0 kWh usable).
    *   *Overnight Direct Consumption:* 1.5 kWh consumed directly off-peak.
    *   *Daytime Discharge:* The battery discharges 9.0 kWh during peak hours.
*   **Annual Arbitrage Balance:**
    *   Stored & Discharged: $9.0 \text{ kWh/day} \times 365 \text{ days} = 3,285 \text{ kWh/year}$ offset from peak.
    *   Grid Import for Storage: $3,285 / 0.90 = 3,650 \text{ kWh/year}$ imported off-peak.
    *   Direct Off-Peak Baseload: 547.5 kWh/year.
    *   Remaining Peak Import: $9,000 - 3,285 - 547.5 = 5,167.5 \text{ kWh/year}$.
*   **Annual Cost:**
    *   Peak Import: $5,167.5 \text{ kWh} \times £0.28 = £1,446.90$
    *   Off-Peak Import: $(3,650 + 547.5) \times £0.085 = £356.79$
    *   Total: £1,803.69
*   **Annual Savings vs. Option 1:** $£2,223.00 - £1,803.69 = £419.31$
*   **Simple Payback:** $£6,500 / £419.31 = \mathbf{15.5 \text{ years}}$
*   **10-Year Net Financial Outcome:** $-£6,500 + (10 \times £419.31) = \mathbf{-£2,306.90}$ (Loss)

### Conclusions & Recommendations

#### 1. Will either battery size pay for itself?
No. Under current 2026 pricing, neither a 5 kWh nor a 10 kWh standalone battery will pay for itself. The simple payback periods (34.9 years and 15.5 years, respectively) far exceed the standard 10-year manufacturer warranties. By the time the systems might break even, the batteries will have degraded significantly or required costly inverter replacements.

#### 2. Does changing tariff without a battery provide savings?
No. Switching to a ToU tariff without a battery would result in a net financial loss (estimated at -£33.75 annually). The household's high, inflexible daytime home office demand prevents them from meeting the 30% to 45% off-peak consumption threshold required to make ToU tariffs profitable [S3, S6].

#### 3. Under what conditions does the conclusion change?
The investment becomes viable if:
*   **Capital costs fall:** A 10 kWh installed system drops below £4,000 [S11].
*   **Tariff spreads widen:** The difference between peak and off-peak rates exceeds £0.25/kWh (e.g., via dynamic smart tariffs) [S8, S9].
*   **Solar is added:** Integrating solar panels allows the battery to be charged for free during summer days, doubling the daily cycling utility and eliminating grid-charging losses.

#### 4. Which option offers the best financial outcome?
**Option 1 (Remaining on a competitive flat-rate tariff)** offers the best financial outcome. It avoids upfront capital risk and protects the household from the premium daytime rates of ToU tariffs.

#### 5. What is the strongest argument against this recommendation?
The strongest counterargument is **energy security and resilience**. If the home office suffers high financial losses from power outages, the backup power capability of a home battery (assuming an EPS/gateway is installed) may provide peace-of-mind value that compensates for the £2,300 net financial loss over 10 years.

#### 6. What data should the homeowner obtain next?
Before making any decisions, the homeowner should:
1.  **Download half-hourly smart meter data (smart tariff readiness):** Use a free service (e.g., Loop or Hugo) to extract exact half-hourly consumption data to map their true load profile.
2.  **Obtain itemized installer quotes:** Request written quotes specifying the exact inverter model, battery chemistry (LFP is preferred for lifespan), round-trip efficiency, and whether the quote includes emergency power supply (EPS) functionality for power cuts.
