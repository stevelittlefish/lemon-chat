# Is a home battery worthwhile without solar panels for my UK home?

Assess whether installing a home battery without solar panels is financially worthwhile for a household in England as of 14 July 2026.

Assume the household:

- Uses approximately 9,000 kWh of electricity per year
- Currently pays a flat rate of approximately £0.247/kWh
- Uses gas for heating
- Has a smart meter and can change electricity tariff
- Has a home office consuming approximately 10–20 kWh per day
- Uses much of the office electricity during daytime working hours
- Can shift only a small amount of consumption manually
- Owns the property and expects to remain there for at least 10 years
- Wants financial savings primarily; backup power is a secondary benefit
- Does not expect to install solar panels during the assessment period

Compare:

1. Remaining on a competitive flat-rate tariff without a battery
2. Switching to a suitable time-of-use tariff without a battery
3. Installing a roughly 5 kWh battery and switching tariff
4. Installing a roughly 10 kWh battery and switching tariff

Consider current installed prices, suitable electricity tariffs, off-peak charging and peak-time discharging, the household’s unusually high daytime demand, usable battery capacity, maximum charge and discharge rates, round-trip losses, degradation, warranties, replacement costs, maintenance, tariff uncertainty, and relevant UK taxes, incentives, or electricity-market rules.

Calculate indicative annual electricity costs, annual savings, simple payback, and the 10-year net financial outcome for each option. Clearly show all assumptions. Include sensitivity analysis for annual usage of **6,000, 9,000, and 11,000 kWh**, as well as changes in tariff price spreads, installation costs, battery degradation, and the proportion of consumption occurring during peak hours.

Do not assume the battery completes a full charge cycle every day unless the evidence and estimated demand profile support that assumption. Account for the possibility that some overnight electricity would be consumed directly rather than stored.

Prefer current primary sources such as energy suppliers, Ofgem, government publications, manufacturers’ technical documentation, and independent trials. Treat installer estimates, affiliate websites, and marketing claims cautiously. Attach citations directly to the claims they support.

Conclude:

- Whether either battery size is likely to pay for itself
- Whether changing tariff without buying a battery would provide meaningful savings
- The conditions under which the conclusion changes
- Which option offers the best financial outcome
- The strongest argument against the recommendation
- What consumption data and quotations the homeowner should obtain before making a purchase
